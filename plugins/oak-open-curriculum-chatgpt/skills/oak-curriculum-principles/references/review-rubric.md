# Reviewing material against the principles

Use this when the task is to **audit or quality-assure** existing material — a unit, lesson, scheme of work, resource, or curriculum sequence — against the six principles. The goal is a fair, specific, actionable review that improves the work, not a gotcha checklist.

## How to review well

- **Judge against intent.** A Year 2 phonics lesson and a Year 11 essay scheme are held to the same principles but look completely different. Ask whether the material serves _its_ pupils and purpose, not whether it matches a template.
- **Evidence over opinion.** Point to the specific slide, task, or sequence that prompts each judgement. "Slide 7 introduces 'isotope' but never defines it" beats "vocabulary is weak."
- **Strengths first, then growth.** Lead with what works. Most material is someone's careful effort; the review should make it better, not smaller.
- **Respect autonomy.** These are _guiding_ principles and the model is optional. Frame gaps as opportunities and offer options, not mandates. The teacher knows their pupils.
- **Prioritise.** Don't list twenty equal points. Surface the two or three changes that would most improve pupil learning.

## Rating language

Avoid harsh numeric scores. For each principle, judge:

- **Secure** — the principle is clearly and consistently evident.
- **Developing** — partly evident; specific, achievable improvements would strengthen it.
- **Not yet evident** — largely absent; needs attention before use.
- **N/A** — genuinely not applicable to this artefact (say why).

## The audit: questions per principle

Work through each principle. For each, the **probe questions** drive the judgement; the **strong/weak signals** help you place it.

### 1. Knowledge and vocabulary rich

- Probe: What specific knowledge will a pupil hold afterwards? Which vocabulary is taught (not just used)? Are keywords defined precisely? Is new knowledge linked to prior knowledge?
- Strong: clear knowledge payload per lesson; deliberate keywords with definitions; vocabulary revisited.
- Weak: activity-led with vague learning; words used but never taught; "engagement" substituting for content; generic skills with no knowledge to apply them to.

### 2. Sequenced and coherent

- Probe: Does each step build on secure prior knowledge? Are prerequisites taught first? Which threads does this belong to, and is the vertical link visible? Is prior content retrieved?
- Strong: logical prerequisite order; explicit prior-knowledge links; clear thread membership; built-in retrieval.
- Weak: arbitrary or purely thematic order; concepts before their prerequisites; isolated units; no sense of what comes before or after.

### 3. Evidence-informed

- Probe: Is there a rationale for key choices? Are well-evidenced techniques used (retrieval, spacing, worked examples, dual coding, explicit instruction)? Are misconceptions anticipated with a response?
- Strong: choices justified; cognitive-science-aligned design; named misconceptions with teacher responses.
- Weak: practices the evidence cautions against (learning styles, unguided discovery for novices); unsupported claims; no misconception planning; fashion over evidence.

### 4. Flexible

- Probe: Can a teacher adapt this safely? Is it mapped to the national curriculum? Are threads signposted so adaptation preserves coherence? Is core vs adaptable distinguished?
- Strong: modular; mapped to the national curriculum; threads visible; suggestions not mandates; single ambitious pathway.
- Weak: rigid lock-step; no mapping; adaptation would silently break the sequence; an implied single "correct" delivery.

### 5. Diverse

- Probe: Whose voices and perspectives are present, and whose are missing? Are there both windows and mirrors? Is diversity woven through or bolted on? Is ambition maintained?
- Strong: deliberate, justified selection; genuine windows and mirrors; range of voices without tokenism; breadth _and_ rigour.
- Weak: unexamined default canon; one-off tokenistic inclusion; diversity claimed but not in the content; homogeneous sources; use of gendered language.

### 6. Accessible

- Probe: Can all pupils, including those with SEND, access the same ambitious content? Is content chunked, dual-coded, scaffolded (with scaffolds designed to fade)? Are checks for understanding built in? Are materials themselves accessible (contrast, captions, cognitive load)?
- Strong: accessibility designed in; same destination with scaffolded routes; regular checks; materials meet accessibility guidelines.
- Weak: "differentiation" that lowers expectations for some; inaccessible materials; permanent scaffolds; SEND as an afterthought.

## Check the tensions, too

Beyond each principle individually, check how they interact:

- Has _flexibility_ been bought at the cost of _coherence_ (adaptable but the sequence breaks)?
- Has _accessibility_ been bought at the cost of _ambition_ (accessible but the content is thinned)?
- Has _diversity_ fragmented the _coherence_ (broad but disconnected)?

Strong material resolves these rather than trading one principle away. The tie-breaker question: **does this help all pupils learn powerful knowledge?**

## Writing the review

Structure the output like this:

1. **Summary judgement** — two or three sentences: overall strengths, and the single most valuable improvement.
2. **Principle-by-principle** — each principle with its rating (Secure / Developing / Not yet evident / N/A) and one or two pieces of _specific, located_ evidence.
3. **Priority actions** — the two or three changes that would most improve pupil learning, each phrased as an actionable suggestion with an example.
4. **What to keep** — name the strengths worth protecting, so improvement doesn't erode them.

Keep the tone collegial and concrete. You are helping a professional make a good thing better.

### Output template

Use this exact skeleton so every review is consistent and skimmable. Fill the brackets; keep the principle order; omit a principle only with an explicit _N/A (reason)_.

```markdown
## Review: [material] — against Oak's curriculum principles

**Summary judgement.** [2–3 sentences: main strengths + the single highest-value change.]

| Principle                   | Rating                                | Evidence (located)       |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Knowledge & vocabulary rich | Secure / Developing / Not yet evident | [section + what you saw] |

| Diverse                     | …                                     | …                        |

1. [Most valuable change — specific, with an example.]

```

## Worked micro-example

> **Material reviewed:** a Year 8 history lesson, "The Transatlantic Slave Trade — causes."
>
> **Summary judgement.** Strong knowledge content and a clear enquiry framing; the single highest-value change is to define and pre-teach the three keywords before the source analysis, so the disciplinary task rests on secure vocabulary.
>
> **Knowledge & vocabulary rich — Developing.** Rich substantive content (slides 2–6), but "abolition", "triangular trade" and "chattel" appear in the source task (slide 9) without having been defined. _Action: add a vocabulary cycle before slide 9, with pupil-facing definitions and an example._
>
> **Sequenced & coherent — Secure.** Sits clearly in the "Empire, persecution and resistance" thread; recaps prior learning on transatlantic geography (slide 2).
>
> **Diverse — Developing.** Centres enslaved people's experiences (good), but interpretation work uses a single historian. _Action: add a second, contrasting interpretation to model that interpretations are constructed._
>
> **Keep:** the enquiry question and the primary-source sequencing — both strong.

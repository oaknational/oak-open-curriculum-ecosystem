# Applying the principles when creating

Use this when the task is to **design or write** something new: a curriculum sequence, a unit, a lesson, an assessment, or a resource. The six principles are the design constraints; this file is the workflow for turning them into a coherent artefact, plus worked examples.

## Work at the right grain

The principles apply at every level, but they bite differently depending on what you're making. Identify the grain first.

| Grain                     | What it is                                  | Where the principles concentrate                                                                                 |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Curriculum sequence**   | What is taught, in what order, across years | Sequenced & coherent (threads, prerequisites); diverse (selection); flexible (mapped to the national curriculum) |
| **Unit**                  | A coherent block of lessons on a topic      | All six; especially sequencing within the unit and its place in a thread                                         |
| **Lesson**                | A single teaching session                   | Knowledge & vocabulary rich; evidence-informed (misconceptions, retrieval); accessible                           |
| **Resource / assessment** | Slides, worksheet, quiz, task               | Knowledge-rich; accessible (cognitive load); evidence-informed (assessment design)                               |

## A design workflow

Adapt the depth to the grain, but the spine is the same.

1. **Establish purpose and place.** What is this for, and where does it sit? For a unit or lesson: which thread(s) does it belong to, and what comes immediately before and after? Map it to the national curriculum (or your equivalent statutory framework) up front — this is what makes it adaptable later.

2. **Specify the knowledge and vocabulary.** Write down the substantive knowledge a pupil should hold afterwards, as a short list of **key learning points**. Choose the **keywords** deliberately and define each precisely in pupil-facing language. This list _is_ the backbone; everything else serves it.

3. **Sequence for secure prerequisites.** Order the content so nothing is required before it has been taught. Note the prior knowledge each step assumes and plan to retrieve it. If a prerequisite is missing from earlier in the curriculum, that is a sequencing problem to fix, not to paper over.

4. **Anticipate misconceptions.** For the key ideas, name the misconceptions and common mistakes pupils typically bring or form, and design the response — a diagnostic question, a representation, a worked example. (Oak lessons store these as a _misconception → teacher response_ pair; do the same.)

5. **Design for access without lowering the ceiling.** Chunk content; use dual coding (clear visuals with the words); model with worked examples; build in checks for understanding; add scaffolds you intend to remove. Keep the destination identical for all pupils; vary the support, not the goal.

6. **Make deliberate, diverse selections.** Where you choose texts, examples, sources, or contexts, choose for both windows and mirrors, and be ready to say why. Diversity is part of quality, not a quota bolted on at the end.

7. **Build in flexibility.** Mark what is core and what is adaptable. Signpost threads, and how the content maps to the national curriculum, so a teacher can localise a context or swap an example without breaking the sequence. Offer suggestions ("you might…") rather than mandates.

8. **Write the rationale.** Capture, briefly, _why_ it is designed this way against each principle. Use `assets/curriculum-rationale-template.md`. A rationale is what lets others adapt with confidence — and it is the best defence against drift.

## Concrete moves, principle by principle

- **Knowledge & vocabulary rich** → key learning points list; keyword glossary with definitions; planned repetition of vocabulary across the sequence.
- **Sequenced & coherent** → prerequisite map; thread tagging; retrieval of prior thread content at the start.
- **Evidence-informed** → misconception/response pairs; retrieval and spacing built into the sequence; worked examples for new procedures; a one-line rationale per major choice.
- **Flexible** → mapping to the national curriculum; core-vs-adaptable markers; "you might adapt this for your context by…" notes.
- **Diverse** → an explicit, justified selection of texts/examples/voices; windows and mirrors audit.
- **Accessible** → chunking; dual coding; faded scaffolds; checks for understanding; accessible formatting (contrast, captions, load).

## Worked example A — designing a unit

_(For the data-grounded version of this example — real keywords, the documented misconception, and the thread order pulled from Oak — see the `oak-curriculum-principles-mcp-enabled` skill.)_

> **Brief:** a Year 5 maths unit on adding and subtracting fractions.
>
> 1. **Place:** belongs to the _Number: Fractions_ thread; follows units on unit/non-unit fractions and equivalence; precedes mixed-number operations. Mapped to the relevant national curriculum statements.
> 2. **Knowledge & vocabulary:** key learning points include "fractions with the same denominator can be added by adding the numerators"; keywords _numerator, denominator, unit(ising)_ with definitions.
> 3. **Sequence:** same-denominator addition → same-denominator subtraction → bridging to non-unit fractions → simple mixed numbers. Each lesson retrieves the prior step.
> 4. **Misconception:** pupils often add both numerators _and_ denominators. Response: build "unitising" through concrete objects first, then connect to notation, then a diagnostic question that surfaces the error.
> 5. **Access:** concrete–pictorial–abstract progression; bar models as the shared representation; worked example then guided practice; same content for all, with extra concrete support where needed.
> 6. **Flexible:** the watermelon/real-object context is marked adaptable; teachers can localise it. Core mathematics is marked fixed.

## Worked example B — designing a single lesson

> **Brief:** a Year 8 science lesson introducing cells.
>
> - **Place:** in the biology "what are living things" thread; assumes prior learning that organisms carry out life processes — retrieve this in the starter.
> - **Knowledge & vocabulary:** key learning points on cells as the basic unit of life; keywords _cell, organelle, microscope_ defined precisely.
> - **Evidence-informed:** known misconception that cells are "blobs" with no internal structure → address with a labelled model and a comparison task. Exit quiz checks the key learning points.
> - **Access:** clear labelled diagrams (dual coding), one new idea per chunk, a check for understanding after each.
> - **Diverse / flexible:** the examples of organisms can be localised; the core biology stays fixed.

## A note on tone and authorship

If you are _writing_ pupil- or teacher-facing copy as part of the resource, keep it clear, warm and free of jargon. If the output is Oak-branded, follow Oak's brand and editorial guidance. If it is independent, the principles still hold — just without Oak's specific voice and visual identity.

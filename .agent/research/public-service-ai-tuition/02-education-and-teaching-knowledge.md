# Education and teaching knowledge

**Purpose:** give OCE readers a rigorous account of worthwhile education and the knowledge and enacted capability needed to support it, before selecting service features or engineering mechanisms. **Standing:** source-based synthesis and bounded design guidance. **Evidence dates:** 4–5 September 2026; reconstructed 6 September 2026 from the admitted source snapshots, without a fresh literature search.

Good education deliberately and equitably develops worthwhile knowledge, understanding, capability, judgement and agency through coherent curriculum, subject-sensitive responsive teaching, valid assessment, ambitious and relationally supportive environments, expert educators and institutions able to learn from trustworthy evidence. Oral, printed, practical, digital and mixed settings can each support these ends. A delivery medium supplies possibilities and constraints; it does not establish educational value.

This document owns the independent educational account, its evidence distinctions, teaching-knowledge taxonomy and learning-to-learn capabilities. ## Purposes and success

Educational rigour asks whether the enterprise has:

- explicit and publicly defensible purposes;
- a justified account of what learners are entitled to know, understand, value and be able to do;
- breadth across domains and depth within them;
- coherent progression rather than a collection of activities or resources;
- attention to disciplinary forms of evidence, reasoning and practice;
- alignment among curriculum, teaching and assessment;
- representation of the knowledge and experiences of a plural society;
- feasibility within real time, staffing and workload constraints; and
- mechanisms for review as knowledge, society and learners change.

This is partly empirical and partly normative. Research can show consequences of curricular choices, but it cannot decide by experiment alone which histories deserve study, what civic obligations education should cultivate, or how a society should balance individual flourishing, economic participation, democratic life, culture and ecological responsibility. UNESCO’s curriculum work treats a quality curriculum as an equitable public entitlement connecting purposes, content, pedagogy, assessment, implementation and evaluation; its wider futures work explicitly frames education as a public endeavour and common good. [UNESCO-IBE, *What Makes a Quality Curriculum?*](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000243975); [UNESCO, *Reimagining Our Futures Together*](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000379707)

#### Pedagogical rigour

Pedagogical rigour asks whether teaching is based on a plausible, inspectable account of learning rather than surface performance. It requires:

- diagnosis of prior knowledge, misconceptions and language;
- instructional choices appropriate to the subject, task and learner’s current expertise;
- explanations, representations, examples and questions selected for a reason;
- sufficient cognitive activity by the learner;
- guided practice and feedback followed by an opportunity to act;
- cumulative review and checks for retention and transfer;
- adaptation from evidence without lowering the intellectual destination;
- attention to motivation, identity, belonging and participation; and
- teacher judgement about when to explain, prompt, model, question, discuss, practise, withdraw support or reteach.

The National Academies’ consensus synthesis is a useful anchor: learning involves cognition, motivation, emotion, social interaction, culture and biology; prior knowledge shapes interpretation; memory and metacognition matter; and effective environments support belonging, agency, purpose and competence while engaging learners in disciplinary practices. It explicitly rejects designing for an imaginary “average learner.” [National Academies, *How People Learn II*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24783)

### A multidimensional account of successful learning

Immediate task performance is not identical to learning. Engagement is not identical to learning. Curriculum coverage, course completion, test scores, credentials and employability each reveal something, but none is an adequate general definition.

The National Academies’ synthesis [*How People Learn II*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24783/chapter/2) treats learning as lasting adaptation across interacting cognitive, biological, social and cultural systems. It stresses prior knowledge, motivation, self-regulation, context, culture and the fit between pedagogy and learning goal. It also cautions that no technology or method is ideal independent of the learner, purpose and setting.

For this project, successful learning should be treated as a profile with at least eight dimensions:

1. **Durability:** knowledge, skill or disposition remains available beyond the immediate performance.
2. **Understanding and organisation:** the learner has a connected model, not only isolated responses.
3. **Use and transfer:** learning can be selected and adapted in relevant new situations, with appropriate limits.
4. **Epistemic quality:** the learner can judge evidence, sources, uncertainty and the grounds for knowing.
5. **Increasing self-direction:** the learner can plan, monitor, evaluate, seek help and revise strategies.
6. **Motivation, identity and agency:** learning expands rather than systematically diminishes the desire and perceived capacity to continue.
7. **Social, ethical and civic formation:** learning supports participation with others, responsible judgement and the purposes education publicly values.
8. **Equity and wellbeing:** success is not purchased through exclusion, humiliation, surveillance, preventable harm or the transfer of unreasonable burdens to the learner.

Different educational purposes weight these dimensions differently. A safety-critical procedure requires reliable performance; a history curriculum requires knowledge and evidential judgement; democratic education raises public and ethical purposes. A standard should therefore represent **outcome profiles and trade-offs**, not collapse success into a single score.


## Knowledge and enacted capability

Lee Shulman’s foundational account of teacher knowledge distinguished content knowledge, general pedagogical knowledge, curriculum knowledge, pedagogical content knowledge, knowledge of learners, educational contexts, and educational purposes and values. Its enduring contribution was the recognition that knowing a subject and knowing how to make it learnable are not the same. The categories below extend that insight for a contemporary knowledge infrastructure. ([Shulman, *Knowledge and Teaching*](https://www.harvardeducationalreview.org/content/57/1/1))

| Kind of knowledge or capability | Core question | Example in science | Why it cannot be replaced by subject knowledge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subject knowledge | What is true, warranted or practised in this domain? | Particle models, experimental control, scientific argument | Expertise can be tacit and organised very differently from a novice’s knowledge. |
| Disciplinary and epistemic knowledge | How does this field produce, test and revise knowledge? | What counts as evidence for a causal claim? | Facts alone do not teach how scientific knowledge is made or challenged. |
| Curriculum knowledge | What should be learned, in what broad progression and for what ends? | How forces are revisited across phases | A curriculum selects and sequences; it does not determine the teaching response to these pupils now. |
| Pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) | How can this particular idea be made learnable to particular learners? | Using contrasting cases to distinguish mass and weight | This joins subject structure to likely misconceptions, representations, tasks and explanations. |
| General pedagogical knowledge | What supports participation, attention, practice, discussion and classroom order across subjects? | Establishing routines for practical work | Some teaching demands cut across subjects but remain sensitive to context. |
| Knowledge of learners and development | What do these learners know, need, value and experience? | Language demands, working memory, prior conceptions, sensory access | A logically sound explanation may still be inaccessible, mistimed or alienating. |
| Assessment knowledge | What evidence would reveal current understanding and guide the next decision? | A diagnostic question that separates two misconceptions | Completion, confidence and correct answers can be poor proxies for the underlying model. |
| Adaptive and inclusive expertise | How should teaching vary without lowering the intellectual goal? | Alternative representations and equipment access while preserving the scientific construct | Uniform provision is not equitable provision. |
| Relational and ethical capability | What relationship, authority and duty of care make learning possible and legitimate? | Responding to error without humiliation | Trust, safety, belonging and justice are conditions of learning, not decorative extras. |
| Implementation and organisational knowledge | What can work reliably here, with these people, resources and constraints? | Laboratory time, technician capacity, teacher expertise | An efficacious intervention can fail when its mechanisms are not reproduced. |
| Metacognitive and lifelong-learning knowledge | How does the learner increasingly plan, monitor, evaluate and redirect their own learning? | Selecting a strategy, checking understanding and seeking help | Independence is not produced merely by exposure to content. |
| Enacted capability | Can the person notice, decide, act, observe and adapt in real time? | Abandoning a planned explanation after a diagnostic response | Propositional knowledge is not equivalent to fluent professional performance. |

This final distinction—between knowledge and capability—is decisive. Teaching is a responsive practice under conditions of incomplete information. A repository can support it, but no inventory of propositions by itself creates judgement, perception, relationship, timing or practical fluency.

### Subject-specific teaching knowledge

Generic principles become education only through subject-specific enactment. Pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) includes the progression of ideas, powerful examples and representations, typical misconceptions, explanatory choices, task design and questions that reveal understanding. Reviews and primary studies consistently associate stronger PCK with instructional quality and pupil outcomes, though measures vary and much of the evidence is correlational. [Fukaya et al., 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1435758/full); [Grigaliūnienė et al., 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11858-025-01684-1); [Baumert et al., 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0002831209345157)

Subject research supplies more useful specificity than generic method labels:

- **Early reading:** systematic decoding/phonics is important but insufficient; phonemic awareness, fluency, vocabulary, oral language and comprehension must develop together. [Castles, Rastle & Nation, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29890888/); [IES Foundational Skills Practice Guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/21)
- **Mathematics:** conceptual understanding and procedural fluency are mutually supportive; modelling, representations, reasoning, practice, questioning and cumulative review all matter, with the balance changing by topic and expertise. [National Mathematics Advisory Panel, 2008](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED500486.pdf); [IES Mathematics Practice Guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/26)
- **Science:** disciplinary knowledge and scientific practices should be intertwined; inquiry needs appropriate guidance. [National Research Council, *Framework for K–12 Science Education*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/publication/13165); [Furtak et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654312457206)
- **Writing:** effective instruction combines meaningful composition, explicit strategies, transcription, process, feedback, peer assistance and self-regulation. [Graham et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11145-024-10539-2)


## Where teaching knowledge is found

### A distributed professional knowledge base

In England, the [Initial Teacher Training and Early Career Framework](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/initial-teacher-training-and-early-career-framework) provides a national minimum entitlement expressed as “learn that” and “learn how to.” It covers high expectations, how pupils learn, subject and curriculum, classroom practice, adaptive teaching, assessment, behaviour and professional behaviours. The Education Endowment Foundation (EEF) assessed its evidence base, while providers are expected to turn the framework into sequenced, subject- and phase-relevant professional learning. It is an important source of common professional knowledge, not a complete model of teacher expertise.

Other prominent sources include:

- [EEF guidance reports](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports) and the [Teaching and Learning Toolkit](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit), which synthesise evidence and offer implementation recommendations while warning against treating average effects as recipes for every setting;
- Ofsted’s [curriculum research reviews](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/curriculum-research-reviews), which assemble research into inspectorial accounts of high-quality education by subject; the [science review](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/research-review-series-science), for example, considers curriculum, pedagogy, assessment and systems;
- subject-specific professional and research communities, such as the [NCETM mastery professional-development materials](https://www.ncetm.org.uk/teaching-for-mastery/mastery-materials/primary-mastery-professional-development/), the Institute of Physics’ research-informed [misconceptions resources](https://spark.iop.org/misconceptions), and the Royal Society of Chemistry’s open-access journal [*Chemistry Education Research and Practice*](https://www.rsc.org/publishing/journals/chemistry-education-research-and-practice);
- cross-subject syntheses such as the [Great Teaching Toolkit evidence review](https://evidencebased.education/the-great-teaching-toolkit-evidence-review/), which groups evidence around understanding content, creating a supportive environment, maximising opportunities to learn and activating hard thinking; and
- research-to-practice infrastructures such as the US What Works Clearinghouse [practice guides](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguides), which combine systematic evidence review, expert judgement and practitioner relevance.

These sources overlap but are not interchangeable. A statutory framework has a different authority from a meta-analysis. A subject association can possess deeper didactic knowledge than a generic evidence toolkit. A research review can estimate an average causal effect without explaining how an expert teacher recognises the moment to act. Practitioner and learner knowledge may reveal feasibility, meaning or harm that an efficacy study did not measure.

### Oak as curriculum and resource infrastructure

Oak’s [curriculum guidance](https://www.thenational.academy/a-guide-to-our-new-curricula) explains subject principles, sequencing, prior knowledge, curriculum threads and “why this, why now”; its [lesson-planning support](https://www.thenational.academy/lesson-planning) and teacher-facing lesson materials embody choices about explanation, modelling, questioning, practice and assessment. Oak also says that its curricula are optional and adaptable and that teachers must respond to their schools and pupils.

Oak is principally a curriculum-and-resource infrastructure. Much of its pedagogical knowledge is:

- **embedded** in sequences, slides, scripts, quizzes and videos rather than stated as separately addressable claims;
- **local** to a resource rather than connected to a general, subject-specific knowledge graph;
- **human-readable** rather than available through a public schema for pedagogical rationale, conditions, mechanisms and evidence;
- **selective**, because a resource necessarily instantiates one design among defensible alternatives; and
- **not a general standard of teaching expertise**, nor a complete account of how teachers diagnose and adapt in live classrooms.

This is not a special criticism of Oak. It is the normal state of curriculum publishing. Resources are often the containers in which pedagogical knowledge travels, but their underlying reasoning is difficult to inspect, compare, contest, reuse or update independently.

Her publicly visible work is not a large programme of peer-reviewed causal research, and it should not be represented as one. #### Why her “subject knowledge for teaching” framework matters

McCrea’s distinction between knowing mathematics and knowing it **for teaching** is well founded. A teacher must understand not only a correct procedure, but:

- where it sits in a curriculum and what comes before and after;
- which representations expose structure and which may mislead;
- different solution methods and when each is useful;
- common misconceptions and the reasoning that produces them; and
- questions and tasks that make pupil thinking visible.

This is close to the centre of contemporary PCK research. It also supplies a good test for any curriculum resource: does it merely contain correct material, or does it encode progression, representation, misconception and diagnostic judgement?

The proper evidential statement is therefore: **McCrea offers a credible, usable synthesis that is consonant with stronger external research.** The stronger causal or correlational evidence comes from the PCK, learning-science, curriculum and assessment literatures; it should not be transferred automatically to every strategy in her books or to Oak’s complete curriculum.


## Curriculum and pedagogical practice

There is no global regulator of pedagogy. The closest thing to an establishment standard is the overlap among major consensus reports, professional frameworks, evidence reviews and system guidance.

OECD’s 2025 synthesis organises high-quality teaching into five dimensions: cognitive engagement, quality subject-matter content, social-emotional support, classroom interaction, and formative assessment and feedback. It identifies 20 practices but cautions against treating them as a checklist. The Great Teaching Toolkit reaches a closely related four-part structure: understand the content and how it is learned; create a supportive environment; maximise opportunity to learn; and activate hard thinking. Both treat teaching as informed professional work, not compliance with a script. [OECD, *Unlocking High-Quality Teaching*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/04/unlocking-high-quality-teaching_808bd876/full-report.html); [Coe et al., *Great Teaching Toolkit Evidence Review*](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/584544-great-teaching-toolkit-evidence-review-introduction.pdf)

### A coherent educational design

| Dimension | Contemporary standard of good education |
|---|---|
| **Purposes** | A clear account of intellectual, personal, civic, ethical, cultural, vocational and wellbeing aims; unavoidable value choices are open to scrutiny |
| **Entitlement** | Ambitious access to important domains for every learner, with attention to equity and future option value |
| **Curriculum selection** | Content is selected for significance, explanatory power, disciplinary integrity, relevance and representation—not merely because resources already exist |
| **Coherence and progression** | Concepts, knowledge, practices and vocabulary are sequenced cumulatively; prerequisites and likely misconceptions are identified; connections are deliberate |
| **Teaching** | Clear goals, explanation and modelling; guided and independent practice; questioning, dialogue, inquiry and problem-solving calibrated to expertise |
| **Memory and understanding** | Retrieval, spacing, cumulative review, explanation, comparison and application are used to build organised knowledge and flexible use |
| **Assessment** | Measures are valid for their purpose; classroom evidence changes instruction; summative decisions use sufficiently broad and dependable evidence |
| **Motivation and climate** | High expectations coexist with safety, order, belonging, autonomy support, competence, purpose and respectful relationships |
| **Inclusion** | Common ambition plus accessible representations, reasonable adjustments, language support and targeted intervention; progress and participation are monitored |
| **Teacher expertise** | Teachers possess subject knowledge, pedagogical content knowledge, assessment literacy and contextual knowledge of learners |
| **Professional learning** | Sustained, content-focused development, rehearsal, coaching, collaborative inquiry and feedback are supported by time and leadership |
| **Implementation** | Responsibilities, resources, workload, adaptations, costs and feedback loops are designed, not assumed |
| **Evaluation** | Learning, retention, transfer, equity, wellbeing and unintended effects are examined using methods proportionate to the claims |

### Curriculum coherence, memory, instruction and assessment

The strongest current settlement is **breadth across subjects and depth within them**. Curriculum overload should be addressed through focus, intelligible progression and prioritisation, not through premature narrowing. International curriculum mapping shows that systems commonly add critical thinking, problem-solving, learning-to-learn and agency to disciplinary content. This establishes adoption, not effectiveness. [OECD, *Curriculum Overload: A Way Forward*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/curriculum-overload_3081ceca-en.html); [OECD, *What Students Learn Matters*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/what-students-learn-matters_d86d4d9a-en.html)

Curricular coherence means more than ordering topics. Important ideas are revisited with increasing sophistication; prior learning is made usable; horizontal connections do not dissolve subjects into generic themes; assessments sample what the curriculum values; and materials, professional learning and timetables make enactment possible. Comparative evidence associates more focused and coherent mathematics/science standards with stronger systems, but much of it is observational. Learning progressions can improve teacher judgement and instructional decisions, yet many are still expert conjectures rather than longitudinally validated models. [Schmidt, Wang & McKnight, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1080/0022027042000294682); [Harris, Adie & Wyatt-Smith, 2022](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543221081552); [Jin et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/sce.21525)

The knowledge-versus-skills opposition is a false binary. Reasoning, comprehension and transfer depend on organised domain knowledge, while worthwhile knowledge is used in explanation, judgement, practice and creation. A meta-analysis of critical-thinking instruction found a modest positive average effect, with stronger designs generally combining explicit attention to thinking with substantive subject matter. Far transfer remains difficult; neither content-free skills nor inert fact accumulation is an adequate model. [Abrami et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314551063); [Sala & Gobet, 2019](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/5/1/18/113004/Near-and-Far-Transfer-in-Cognitive-Training-A)

Current England policy illustrates this establishment position rather than defining it globally. The 2025 Curriculum and Assessment Review recommends a broad entitlement to age 16, knowledge and skills developed together in subject-appropriate ways, careful progression, room for depth and mastery, and assessment modes derived from subjects. Its conclusions are policy judgements supported by evidence—not causal proof that each recommendation is optimal. [UK Department for Education, *Curriculum and Assessment Review: Final Report*](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/curriculum-and-assessment-review-final-report)

#### Teaching: explicit support and active sense-making

Novices need more guidance because they have fewer organised schemas and limited working-memory capacity. Clear explanation, modelling, worked examples and guided practice reduce avoidable search and make important features visible. As knowledge grows, excessive support can become redundant or obstructive, so prompts, examples and scaffolds should be faded and learners should assume more responsibility. A 2025 synthesis of the expertise-reversal effect found that high assistance benefited learners with low prior knowledge, while lower assistance could benefit those with high prior knowledge. [Cowan, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20445769/); [Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5); [Tetzlaff et al., 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475225000660)

This does not imply passive reception. Learning requires the learner to retrieve, select, organise, explain, compare, infer, practise, discuss or solve. A large higher-education STEM meta-analysis found active learning improved achievement and reduced failure compared with traditional lecturing; it does not show that any activity is superior to any explanation, or that the same effect applies unchanged to every age and subject. Learners may also *feel* they learn less during effortful active lessons even when they learn more. [Freeman et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24821756/); [Deslauriers et al., 2019](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1821936116)

Inquiry and discovery show the same pattern. Unassisted discovery underperforms explicit instruction on average; enhanced or guided discovery can outperform comparison conditions. Guided inquiry produces positive effects on learning activities, successful performance and learning outcomes. Productive failure can help when initial problem-solving is carefully bounded and followed by well-designed consolidation or instruction. The meaningful variable is not the ideological label but the learner’s prior knowledge, the task, the guidance and what happens afterwards. [Alfieri et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017); [Lazonder & Harmsen, 2016](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/meta-analysis-of-inquiry-based-learning-effects-of-guidance/); [Sinha & Kapur, 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105)

Global evidence also cautions against assuming that conclusions from well-resourced systems are sufficient. In low- and middle-income settings, the Global Education Evidence Advisory Panel identifies structured pedagogy, sustained teacher support, teaching at the learner’s current level and strong early-childhood provision among the more dependable investments. These are system packages rather than isolated techniques: appropriate materials, diagnosis, grouping, teacher development and implementation capacity work together. [World Bank/GEEAP, 2025 “Smart Buys” evidence](https://www.worldbank.org/en/brief/2025/10/30/global-education-evidence-advisory-panel)

#### Practice, memory, feedback and metacognition

Retrieval practice and distributed practice are among the best-supported general learning mechanisms. Classroom retrieval studies mostly report positive effects, including transfer beyond identical questions, while a 2025 classroom spacing synthesis found a moderate average effect accompanied by very high heterogeneity. This warrants deliberate cumulative retrieval and spacing—not constant quizzing, decontextualised fact drilling or high-stakes testing. The evidence base also remains disproportionately drawn from WEIRD settings. [Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt, 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9); [Pan & Rickard, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29733621/); [Mawson & Kang, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12189222/)

Interleaving can improve discrimination between problem types or categories, but effects depend strongly on the material. Worked examples have a moderate positive average effect in mathematics, particularly for novices; the goal is to direct attention to structure and reasoning, then move towards completion problems and independent practice. Deliberate practice matters, but practice amount alone explains only part of performance differences and the popular “10,000 hours” rule is not a scientific law. [Brunmair & Richter, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/); [Barbieri et al., 2023](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1364058); [Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24986855/)

Feedback has a positive average effect but extreme variability, including negative effects. Its value depends on information about the task or process, credibility, timing, learner interpretation and a subsequent opportunity to act. Formative assessment is therefore not a quiz frequency, dashboard or feedback-delivery feature. It is a cycle: clarify the intended learning; elicit evidence; interpret it; decide what to do; act; and check again. A 2024 K–12 meta-analysis found a modest average effect; a large English school trial of a sustained formative-assessment programme found a small positive attainment effect. [Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456/); [Yao et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/13803611.2024.2363831); [EEF, Embedding Formative Assessment](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/embedding-formative-assessment)

Metacognitive teaching is best understood as subject-embedded planning, monitoring and evaluation. Teachers make thinking visible, model strategies, prompt learners to select and justify them, and gradually transfer responsibility. A meta-analysis of school interventions found a substantial average effect but wide variation; generic exhortations to “think about your thinking” are not equivalent to this instructional design. [Donker et al., 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X13000420); [EEF, Metacognition and Self-Regulated Learning](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/metacognition)

#### Assessment: valid inference, responsive action and restrained stakes

Rigorous assessment starts with purpose. A reading-fluency probe, a practical performance, an essay, a concept inventory and a national examination can all be useful, but they license different inferences. Assessment quality concerns construct representation, reliability or precision, fairness and accessibility, and the consequences of the proposed use. No single measure can simultaneously maximise authenticity, breadth, comparability, low workload and diagnostic detail.

High-stakes decisions should not rest on one test. Test-based accountability can narrow the taught curriculum and encourage teaching to predictable formats; net effects vary with design and incentives. Conventional grades are also imperfect: they frequently combine attainment with effort, conduct, participation and teacher judgement, creating ambiguity and scope for bias. Multiple samples, clear constructs, moderation and explicit separation of academic attainment from behaviour improve defensibility. [AERA, Position Statement on High-Stakes Testing](https://www.aera.net/About-AERA/AERA-Rules-Policies/Association-Policies/Position-Statement-on-High-Stakes-Testing); [Au, 2007](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X07306523); [Brookhart et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316672069)

### Relational, inclusive and organisational conditions

Warmth and structure are complements. Learners benefit from predictable classrooms, clear expectations, effective management, respectful relationships, belonging, competence and meaningful autonomy. Meta-analytic evidence links teacher–student relationships, classroom structure and school belonging to engagement and achievement. A large multi-site belonging intervention illustrates an important frontier lesson: average effects conceal strong context dependence, with benefits appearing where local conditions afforded belonging and implementation was suitable. [Roorda et al., 2017](https://www.fachportal-paedagogik.de/literatur/vollanzeige.html?FId=eric_ej1156223); [Patall et al., 2023](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/2023_PatallYatesEtAl_Meta_Structure.pdf); [Walton et al., 2023](https://www.greggmuragishi.com/uploads/5/7/1/5/57150559/walton_et_al_2023.pdf)

Social and emotional learning programmes show small positive average effects across many countries and outcomes, including academic performance. This supports deliberate development of social-emotional capacities, not the claim that a stand-alone package can compensate for unsafe schools, weak teaching, poverty or inadequate mental-health services. Whole-school mental-health evidence remains mixed and implementation-sensitive. [Cipriano et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13968); [Whole-school mental-health review, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11807013/)

#### Inclusion, culture and equity

An inclusive system maintains ambitious intellectual destinations while removing unnecessary barriers and supplying targeted support. A large 2026 SEND synthesis found positive average effects for educational interventions but substantial heterogeneity. A separate review found too little credible evidence to declare mainstream or segregated placement universally superior; placement is not itself a pedagogy. Decisions must examine the type of need, quality and intensity of support, participation, belonging and long-term outcomes. [Van Herwegen et al., 2026](https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/rev3.70127); [Campbell Collaboration, Inclusion of Children with SEND](https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/review/inclusion-children-with-special-educational-needs/)

Universal Design for Learning is influential as an accessibility and barrier-reduction framework. Several component ideas are independently sensible or well supported, but controlled evidence for the complete branded package is limited and methodologically weak. Rights to access and reasonable adjustment do not depend on proving a package-level effect. [CAST, UDL Guidelines 3.0](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/); [Almeqdad et al., 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2331186X.2023.2218191); [Bray et al., 2024](https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/bjet.13328)

Culturally responsive and sustaining education is not one replicable treatment. It ranges from accurate representation and inclusive relationships to community co-design and disciplinary reappraisal. The general literature is heterogeneous, but carefully designed ethnic-studies curricula in one US system have produced credible causal gains in attendance, grades, credits, graduation and college enrolment. This is promising evidence for particular designs, not licence to treat cultural responsiveness as a generic effect size. [Aronson & Laughter, 2016](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315582066); [Bonilla, Dee & Penner, 2021](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2026386118)

#### Teacher expertise, professional learning and implementation

Teacher quality cannot be reduced to generic personality or compliance. Subject knowledge, PCK, general pedagogical knowledge, formative judgement, relationships and contextual responsiveness all matter. Professional development has a small average effect on pupil attainment with a prediction interval that includes negative effects; design and implementation are decisive. Coaching has larger average effects on observed instruction and smaller but meaningful effects on achievement, with attenuation at scale. [Öz et al., 2025](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/when-do-teacher-professional-development-interventions-improve-st/); [Kraft, Blazar & Hogan, 2018](https://annenberg.brown.edu/publications/effect-teacher-coaching-instruction-and-achievement-meta-analysis-causal-evidence)

A written curriculum or evidence brief is not an intervention. Teachers must make sense of it, learn relevant content and practices, adapt responsibly, and receive usable feedback. Implementation therefore needs staged exploration, preparation, delivery and sustainment; clarity about core functions versus adaptable forms; attention to workload and opportunity costs; and repeated learning loops. “Fidelity” without understanding can preserve surface features while losing the mechanism, whereas unlimited local adaptation can dissolve the intervention. [OECD, *Curriculum Reform: A Literature Review to Support Effective Implementation*](https://one.oecd.org/document/EDU/WKP%282020%2927/en/pdf); [EEF, *A School’s Guide to Implementation*](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/implementation)


## What evidence can establish

| Question | What it tells us | What it cannot tell us |
|---|---|---|
| **What is establishment practice?** | The professional standards, frameworks and policy settlements that influential institutions presently endorse | Whether those positions are true, equally implemented or causally effective |
| **What is most adopted?** | What systems prescribe or teachers report doing | The quality, dosage or impact of the practice |
| **What is best supported by evidence?** | Which mechanisms or programmes have credible effects on specified outcomes in specified settings | What education ought ultimately to value, or whether effects transfer to every context |
| **What is at the research frontier?** | Where active, promising inquiry is trying to resolve important limitations | What is already ready for universal implementation |

Collapsing these questions produces bad judgements. A practice may be ubiquitous but ineffective; effective but uncommon; ethically required despite sparse experimental evidence; or promising but not yet established. “Evidence-based” is not a synonym for fashionable, standardised, measurable or technologically sophisticated.

### Match the method to the claim

A rigorous educational claim should answer:

1. **Construct:** What exactly is meant by learning, engagement, belonging, quality or expertise?
2. **Theory:** Through what mechanism should the intervention change it?
3. **Design:** Is the method suitable for the question? A causal claim needs a credible counterfactual; an interpretive question may require qualitative or humanities-based inquiry.
4. **Measurement:** Are outcomes valid, reliable, fair and aligned without simply reproducing the taught task?
5. **Analysis:** Were baseline differences, attrition, missing data, clustering, multiplicity and researcher discretion handled appropriately?
6. **Implementation:** What was actually delivered, by whom, with what adaptation, expertise, time and cost?
7. **Time:** Was learning tested after delay as well as immediately?
8. **Generality:** Does the result replicate across sites, populations, implementers, subjects and outcome measures?
9. **Distribution:** Who benefited, who did not, and were there opportunity costs or harms?
10. **Transparency:** Were protocols, measures, analyses, conflicts and, where ethical, materials/data/code made inspectable?

The methodological establishment is plural rather than exclusively experimental. The American Educational Research Association recognises empirical and humanities-oriented research traditions, while the What Works Clearinghouse supplies detailed standards for judging causal studies. The testing profession’s standards require validity evidence for a particular interpretation and use, reliability or precision, and fairness; a test is not simply “valid” in the abstract. [AERA, Standards for Research Conduct](https://www.aera.net/Publications/Standards-for-Research-Conduct); [What Works Clearinghouse Handbooks, version 5](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/handbooks); [AERA/APA/NCME, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf)

### Authority, evidence, wisdom and representation are separate dimensions

“Rigorous” is not a single property. It must be asked of a particular claim for a particular use. Authority, evidential strength, practical wisdom and machine-readable precision are orthogonal.

| Claim type | Suitable sources and tests | What rigour can establish | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normative: what education should aim at | Democratic deliberation, law, ethics, pupil/community voice, disciplinary argument | Legitimacy, coherence, rights and public purpose | Causal effectiveness does not settle values. |
| Descriptive: what learners or teachers do and experience | Observation, interviews, ethnography, trace data, surveys | Patterns, meanings, practices and contexts | Description alone does not identify causal effects. |
| Causal: whether an intervention changes an outcome | Randomised and strong quasi-experimental studies, systematic review | Average effects under studied conditions | Mechanism, transfer, feasibility and individual benefit may remain uncertain. |
| Mechanistic: how learning or instruction may work | Cognitive, developmental and neuroscience studies; process measures | Plausible processes and boundary conditions | Laboratory effects do not automatically scale to classrooms or curricula. |
| Subject-didactic: how particular content becomes learnable | Discipline-based education research, design studies, misconception research, task analysis | Content-specific representations, sequences and diagnostic insight | Findings may depend strongly on topic, age, culture and enactment. |
| Implementation: how an approach becomes reliable in practice | Process evaluation, realist and mixed-methods research, improvement science | Necessary resources, routines, adaptations and failure modes | Local success does not guarantee transportability. |
| Professional: what expert judgement notices and does | Expertise studies, observation, practitioner inquiry, lesson study, validated consensus | Tacit cues, practical reasoning and feasible action | Consensus can reproduce custom, bias or status unless challenged by other evidence. |
| Measurement: whether an outcome is validly detected | Assessment theory, psychometrics, cognitive diagnosis, validation studies | What inferences scores or observations support | A precise measure can still target the wrong educational end. |

A reliable knowledge infrastructure must keep these forms separate while allowing them to be linked. “Government guidance says,” “three randomised studies found,” “a disciplinary community recommends,” and “pupils report harm” are all potentially decision-relevant, but they are not the same epistemic object.

The EEF Toolkit is a useful model of some of this discipline: it reports average impact, cost and evidence strength, discusses heterogeneity, and explicitly requires professional judgement. The US What Works Clearinghouse goes further in exposing machine-readable study findings: its [open data export](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/studyfindings) includes research ratings, evidence tiers, effect sizes, sample sizes and contextual fields to support replication, meta-analysis and questions about what works for whom. Even this is an evidence substrate, not a complete pedagogical decision system.


## Educational scales and learning-to-learn

Guiding a learner is a nested problem. The relevant knowledge changes with the time horizon.

| Scale | Decisions to be made | Knowledge especially needed |
|---|---|---|
| Moment or conversational turn | What does this response reveal? What should happen next? | Learner meaning, likely misconception, discourse, emotion, diagnostic validity, immediate safety and accessibility. |
| Activity or episode | What task, representation, grouping, scaffold or feedback will advance this goal? | PCK, task demand, cognitive and language load, motivation, participation structure, resource affordances. |
| Lesson | How should goals, explanation, modelling, practice, discussion and assessment cohere? | Curriculum position, prior learning, lesson architecture, classroom routines, formative evidence. |
| Sequence or unit | How should ideas accumulate, vary, revisit and integrate? | Conceptual progression, prerequisites, spacing and retrieval, cumulative assessment, coherent representations. |
| Subject or programme | What forms a powerful and equitable induction into the discipline? | Substantive and disciplinary structures, curriculum breadth/depth, qualification demands, cultural selection, long-term progression. |
| Phase and transitions | What must be sustained or rebuilt across teachers and institutions? | Development, records and privacy, transition knowledge, continuity, credential and pathway structures. |
| Lifelong learning career | How can a person form goals, find trustworthy opportunities, learn across settings and adapt without losing agency? | Self-regulation, epistemic and information literacy, motivation, identity, recognition, networks, time and economic constraints, wellbeing. |

At every scale, guidance needs a common set of knowledge layers:

1. **Purposes and values** — what is being sought and why it is worth seeking.
2. **Outcome model** — what successful learning would look like, including trade-offs and harms.
3. **Learner model** — prior knowledge, conceptions, language, development, goals, motivation, identity, disability, wellbeing and context, without reducing the person to a profile.
4. **Domain model** — concepts, procedures, practices, forms of evidence, disputed knowledge and disciplinary structure.
5. **Curriculum model** — selection, scope, sequence, prerequisites, progression, recurrence and links across domains.
6. **Pedagogical content knowledge** — explanations, examples, analogies, representations, tasks, common conceptions and diagnostic responses for particular content.
7. **General pedagogy and relationships** — participation, discussion, practice, feedback, classroom environment, trust and authority.
8. **Assessment model** — what observation supports which inference, with validity, reliability and consequences.
9. **Adaptive and inclusive knowledge** — barriers, accessibility, language, culture, reasonable adjustment and multiple pathways to the same intellectual goal.
10. **Metacognitive and motivational knowledge** — how the learner becomes more aware, strategic, accurate and self-directing.
11. **Context and implementation** — staffing, time, materials, culture, policy, family/community knowledge and organisational capacity.
12. **Resource and technology knowledge** — affordances, constraints, accessibility, interoperability, privacy, security and opportunity cost.
13. **Evidence and provenance** — claim type, source, population, method, effect, uncertainty, disagreement, recency and applicability.
14. **Professional action knowledge** — cues, routines, rehearsal, judgement, adaptation and reflection needed to turn propositions into competent practice.

No single actor needs to hold every element explicitly at every moment. The infrastructure should make relevant knowledge discoverable and composable while leaving the accountable human able to interpret, refuse and adapt it.

### Increasing learner control within substantive learning

The EEF’s second-edition guidance on [metacognition and self-regulated learning](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/metacognition) reflects a strong evidence base for teaching pupils to plan, monitor and evaluate their learning. Its most important implication is often missed: metacognition is not a content-free superpower. Learners need enough domain knowledge to recognise a problem, select a strategy and judge its success.

A serious learning-to-learn curriculum should develop the following capabilities **inside substantive learning**, then deliberately support transfer:

| Capability | How it is cultivated | Failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Goal formation | Compare purposes, define criteria, decompose tasks and revisit goals | Teaching compliance with assigned goals as if it were agency. |
| Planning | Activate prior knowledge, select strategies, estimate time and anticipate obstacles | Giving strategy lists without situations in which each is useful. |
| Monitoring and calibration | Predict performance, use low-stakes retrieval, explain reasoning and compare confidence with evidence | Equating familiarity or fluent rereading with mastery. |
| Evaluation and adaptation | Review outcomes and processes, diagnose error and change strategy | Reflection rituals with no consequence for the next attempt. |
| Memory and practice management | Use retrieval, spacing, interleaving, worked examples and self-explanation conditionally | Turning useful principles into universal recipes. |
| Epistemic judgement | Trace claims to sources, distinguish observation/inference/value, assess corroboration, uncertainty and conflict | Generic “critical thinking” detached from disciplinary standards of evidence. |
| Information and media literacy | Form queries, navigate, compare, synthesise, cite and maintain provenance | Treating access to information as equivalent to understanding. |
| Help-seeking and collaboration | Recognise impasse, ask productive questions, give and use feedback, participate in knowledge communities | Framing independence as solitary self-sufficiency. |
| Motivation and persistence | Build value, belonging, self-efficacy, autonomy and attainable challenge; manage emotion and recovery | Moralising persistence when the environment is inaccessible or harmful. |
| Transfer | Vary contexts, compare cases, name deep structure and revisit learning across settings | Assuming a skill demonstrated once will spontaneously generalise. |
| Digital and AI judgement | Decide what to delegate, verify outputs, preserve cognitive work that serves the learning goal and disclose assistance | Automating the very thinking the learner needs to develop. |
| Learning ecology | Find people, tools, institutions and opportunities; manage time, resources, credentials and transitions | Locating every failure within the learner rather than their opportunities and constraints. |

The long-term progression is from **other-regulation**, through **co-regulation**, towards increasingly capable **self-regulation**—without pretending that accomplished adults cease to depend on teachers, colleagues, communities or tools. Lifelong learning is distributed across formal, non-formal and informal settings, as UNESCO’s [lifelong-learning framework](https://www.unesco.org/en/lifelong-learning/need-know) emphasises. The [OECD Learning Compass](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/tools/oecd-learning-compass-2030.html) similarly places agency, knowledge, skills, attitudes and values within a broader future-oriented account, while the EU’s [key competences for lifelong learning](https://education.ec.europa.eu/focus-topics/improving-quality/key-competences) combine knowledge, skills and attitudes.


## Supported mechanisms and bounded judgements

The table gives a **confidence judgement**, not a universal ranking. “Strong” means repeated support across credible syntheses for a specified mechanism or family of practices. It does not mean every implementation works or that the reported average effect will reproduce locally.

| Evidence position | Approach or mechanism | Defensible conclusion | Important boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strong** | Building on prior knowledge | Prior knowledge powerfully predicts and shapes new learning | High prior scores may also leave less room for measured gain; diagnosis must be content-specific |
| **Strong** | Explicit explanation, modelling and guidance for novices | Guidance usually beats minimally supported discovery for initial learning | Support should adapt and fade as expertise develops |
| **Strong** | Worked examples and guided practice | Especially useful for novice problem solving and complex procedures | Passive copying is not learning; prompts and transition to independent work matter |
| **Strong** | Retrieval practice | Low-stakes retrieval strengthens later access and often transfer | Quality, spacing, feedback and alignment matter; not a justification for high-stakes test culture |
| **Strong** | Distributed practice | Spacing generally improves long-term retention | Optimal intervals depend on material and retention horizon; classroom estimates vary greatly |
| **Strong, material-dependent** | Interleaving | Can improve discrimination and selection among categories/problem types | Benefits are not universal and blocked practice may be needed during early acquisition |
| **Strong but heterogeneous** | Informative feedback plus use | Feedback can improve learning when learners understand and act on it | Praise, grades, answer revelation and process information behave differently; effects can be negative |
| **Moderate–strong** | Formative assessment cycles | Eliciting and using evidence to adapt teaching produces modest average gains | “More tests” or “more data” is not the mechanism |
| **Moderate–strong** | Subject-embedded metacognition | Modelling and gradually transferring planning, monitoring and evaluation can improve learning | Generic strategy lessons transfer poorly without subject knowledge and practice |
| **Moderate–strong** | Guided inquiry and active/generative learning | Learners benefit from explaining, discussing, solving and investigating with appropriate support | “Active” and “inquiry” are broad labels; learner age, expertise and task structure moderate effects |
| **Moderate–strong** | Clear classroom structure and relationships | Order, expectations, belonging and supportive relationships aid engagement and attainment | Correlational evidence is common; cultural expression and local climate matter |
| **Moderate–strong** | Pedagogical content knowledge | Knowledge of progression, representations and misconceptions supports better teaching | Measures differ and much evidence is correlational |
| **Strong in relevant low-resource settings** | Structured pedagogy and teaching at the learner’s level | Coherent teacher support, usable materials and level-responsive instruction can improve foundational learning | A system package; effects and feasibility depend on local language, capacity and political economy |
| **Moderate, conditional** | Cooperative and dialogic learning | Structured interaction can deepen reasoning and participation | Requires task interdependence, accountability, norms and skilled facilitation |
| **Moderate, conditional** | Mastery instructional cycles | Clear objectives, diagnosis, corrective support and another opportunity can help | Evidence for complete competency-based systems and pace-by-demonstration is much thinner |
| **Moderate, programme-specific** | Social and emotional learning | Well-implemented programmes yield small positive academic and social-emotional effects | Cannot substitute for good teaching, relationships, material security or clinical care |
| **Promising, localised** | Culturally responsive/sustaining curricula | Some rigorous local studies show multi-outcome benefits | Designs are heterogeneous and need replication without stripping local/disciplinary integrity |
| **Thin as a whole package** | Universal Design for Learning | Useful accessibility principles; several components are defensible | Package-level causal evidence is not commensurate with its adoption and claims |
| **Thin/contested** | Competency-based education, ungrading, standards-based grading | Plausible rationales and local successes exist | Long-term comparative evidence on learning, equity and workload is insufficient |
| **Unsupported as prescribed** | Matching instruction to “learning styles” | No adequate evidence for the matching hypothesis | Preferences and accessibility needs are real but do not validate fixed style labels |
| **Unsupported/overclaimed** | Minimally guided discovery as a default | Unguided search is inefficient for most novices | Guided discovery and later independent inquiry are different propositions |
| **Unsupported/overclaimed** | Generic “brain-based” products and neuromyths | Neuroscience can constrain theory but rarely prescribes a classroom method directly | “Left/right brained,” fixed styles and simplistic brain claims are red flags |
| **Overclaimed as a panacea** | Growth-mindset-only intervention | Effects are small, heterogeneous and contested; context and opportunity matter | A message about effort cannot repair weak instruction or structural constraint |

Evidence against matching instruction to declared “learning styles” is longstanding and remains decisive. Neuromyths persist among educators despite professional development. Recent reviews of school-based growth-mindset trials find much smaller and less stable effects than popular accounts imply; competing meta-analyses differ, so the reasonable conclusion is “small and contextual,” not “nothing” or “transformative.” [Pashler et al., 2008](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/); [Jolles & Jolles, 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/mbe.12414); [Growth mindset critical review, 2026](https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/rev3.70066)

### Resolve choices at the mechanism, subject and context

| False choice | Better research-informed formulation |
|---|---|
| **Knowledge or skills** | Organised disciplinary knowledge enables reasoning; practices make knowledge usable. Build both together. |
| **Teacher-led or student-centred** | Teachers design conditions and adapt support so pupils do increasingly consequential intellectual work. |
| **Explicit instruction or inquiry** | Explain and model what learners cannot efficiently discover; use guided inquiry to apply, connect and extend. |
| **Memory or understanding** | Understanding depends on accessible knowledge; retrieval should serve organised meaning, not disconnected recall. |
| **Challenge or support** | Maintain ambitious destinations while varying examples, representations, prompts, time and targeted support. |
| **Academic learning or wellbeing** | Safety, belonging and relationships support learning; intellectual competence and success can support wellbeing. Neither erases the other. |
| **Fidelity or adaptation** | Preserve core functions and mechanisms; adapt forms responsibly using evidence and document the change. |
| **Formative or summative assessment** | Use each for its purpose; do not turn every classroom check into a grade or infer diagnosis from a broad summative score. |
| **Subjects or interdisciplinarity** | Develop disciplinary foundations and make purposeful connections where integration deepens rather than blurs understanding. |
| **Evidence or professional judgement** | Evidence informs the repertoire and estimates risks; skilled teachers interpret it in relation to subject, pupil and context. |


## Adoption is a separate question

Adoption is measured imperfectly. Policy documents describe intended curricula; teacher surveys rely on self-report; observation captures limited lessons; and the same named practice may differ radically in quality.

#### Reported classroom practice

OECD TALIS 2024 surveyed roughly 280,000 educators across 55 education systems. Among lower-secondary teachers:

| Reported practice | International prevalence |
|---|---:|
| Explain what pupils are expected to learn | **91%** |
| Connect new and previous topics | **87%** |
| Set goals at the beginning of instruction | **83%** |
| Re-explain when pupils struggle | **about 90%** |
| Take account of prior knowledge | **about 90%** |
| Present tasks of graduated difficulty | **80%** |
| Give similar tasks until all pupils understand | **70%** |
| Use peer critique | **44%** |
| Give tasks with no obvious solution | **37%** |
| Run projects lasting at least a week | **28%** |

The pattern is clearer than the precise ordering: teacher clarity, connection to prior learning, explanation and structured practice are widely reported; extended projects, open problems and peer critique are less common. These are self-reports, not observations of quality or evidence of effect. [OECD, *TALIS 2024: Teaching for Today’s World*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/10/results-from-talis-2024_28fbde1d/full-report/teaching-for-today-s-world_eefb146b.html)

Professional collaboration is also common in weak forms and rarer in intensive forms. TALIS reports approximately 3.1 hours a week on teamwork and professional dialogue and 86% of teachers saying they can rely on colleagues, but only 9% report observing colleagues and giving feedback at least monthly. [OECD, *Professional Relationships in School Communities*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/results-from-talis-2024_90df6235-en/full-report/professional-relationships-in-school-communities_b6ddef33.html)

#### Intended curriculum

OECD mapping found cross-cutting competency language widely present: critical thinking in 66% of mapped curricula, problem-solving in 59%, learning-to-learn in 36%, learner agency in 33% and co-agency in 27%. Subjects remain the principal organisational structure in most systems. The frontier rhetoric is therefore more competency-oriented than typical classroom enactment, but prevalence does not show that generic competencies have been taught or transferred successfully. [OECD, *What Students Learn Matters*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/what-students-learn-matters_d86d4d9a-en.html); [OECD Learning Compass 2030](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/tools/oecd-learning-compass-2030.html)


## Research traditions and frontiers

Education research is an ecosystem of traditions addressing different questions. Treating one tradition as the whole science creates blind spots.

| Research tradition | Central questions | Characteristic contribution | Characteristic blind spot |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive and experimental learning science** | How do attention, memory, prior knowledge, practice and metacognition change learning? | Mechanisms such as retrieval, spacing, worked examples and cognitive load | Short tasks, proximal outcomes and controlled settings may underrepresent culture and classroom complexity |
| **Developmental science and early childhood** | How do language, play, self-regulation, relationships and development interact? | Age-sensitive accounts and guided-play evidence | Developmental averages can become deterministic stage claims |
| **Subject didactics/PCK and discipline-based education research** | How is particular content understood, represented and misconceived? | High instructional specificity and authentic disciplinary outcomes | Findings may not generalise beyond the subject/topic |
| **Sociocultural, cultural-historical and participation research** | How do identity, language, tools, power and community shape learning? | Explains participation, meaning, context and inequity | Often less able or less intended to estimate causal average effects |
| **Motivation, affect and relationship research** | How do value, expectancy, autonomy, competence, emotion and belonging shape engagement? | Connects learning to climate, identity and persistence | Self-report, correlational designs and construct overlap are common |
| **Assessment and psychometrics** | What inferences do tasks and scores support, with what error and fairness? | Validity, measurement models, comparability and diagnostic design | What is measurable can displace what is educationally valuable |
| **Causal policy research and economics** | What are the average and distributional effects of programmes and policies? | Counterfactual designs, cost and scale | Treatment labels may be coarse; mechanisms and lived experience can disappear |
| **Implementation and improvement science** | How do interventions become routine, adapt and improve in systems? | Context, process, feedback loops, organisational learning and research–practice partnerships | Causal identification is sometimes weaker; local learning may not transport |
| **Educational neuroscience** | How do neural development and learning processes relate? | Biological constraints and convergent mechanism evidence | Large inference gap from brain measure to classroom prescription |
| **Philosophy, history and curriculum theory** | What is education for, whose knowledge counts, and what is just? | Clarifies values, concepts, power and historical contingency | Does not by itself estimate programme effects |

The most mature position is methodological pluralism with claim discipline:

- use experiments and quasi-experiments for credible causal effects;
- use measurement research to establish what outcomes mean;
- use qualitative and design research to understand mechanism, experience and enactment;
- use subject scholarship to protect disciplinary integrity;
- use philosophy and democratic deliberation for questions of purpose and justice;
- use implementation research to examine scale, adaptation and sustainability; and
- triangulate rather than allowing one favourable measure to settle the case.

#### What the accumulated literature reveals

Five broad diagnoses recur:

1. **Mechanisms travel better than brands.** “Retrieve important content after delay” is more testable than “use a revolutionary pedagogy.”
2. **Effects are distributions, not constants.** Comparator quality, age, prior knowledge, subject, dosage, outcome, timing, implementer and local conditions change the result.
3. **Immediate performance is overmeasured.** Fluency, completion, satisfaction and engagement during instruction can diverge from retained learning.
4. **Whole-system evidence is thinner than component evidence.** Coherent combinations may matter most, but they are harder to randomise, describe and replicate.
5. **The literature is geographically and culturally skewed.** Non-WEIRD, multilingual, low-resource and marginalised populations remain underrepresented.

This is why effect-size league tables are dangerous. A single mean collapses intervention variation, comparison conditions, measurement quality, implementation and population. The right question is not “what works?” but “what changed, for whom, compared with what, on which outcome, after how long, under what conditions, at what cost?”

### The research frontier

The strongest current frontiers are not yet universal standards. They are programmes of inquiry attempting to solve limitations in the existing evidence.

#### Heterogeneity without labelling learners

Researchers increasingly examine variation in treatment effects, learner trajectories and context rather than assuming a single average learner. The objective is useful adaptation based on current knowledge, response and circumstance—not permanent typologies such as ability tracks or learning styles. Individual-participant-data meta-analysis, multi-site trials and repeated measures are promising tools, but “personalisation” must avoid turning noisy predictions into lowered expectations.

#### Adaptive expertise and the fading of support

We know more about helping novices acquire routines than about developing flexible expertise. Frontier work asks how examples, prompts and feedback should fade; when productive struggle aids later transfer; how learners recognise which strategy applies; and how to avoid both premature independence and permanent scaffolding.

#### Durable transfer and future learning

Near transfer is commoner than far transfer. Research is moving toward delayed tests, novel problems, preparation for future learning and longitudinal trajectories. The challenge is to develop richly organised disciplinary knowledge that learners can recognise, retrieve and reconfigure in new contexts, rather than training either narrow routines or generic slogans.

#### Integrating cognitive and sociocultural accounts

Cognition does not occur outside culture, language, identity and relationships; participation does not occur without memory, knowledge and attention. A serious frontier connects these levels: how culturally meaningful examples affect encoding and inference; how belonging changes engagement with difficulty; how classroom discourse shapes conceptual development; and how material and institutional conditions change opportunity to learn.

#### Motivation, agency and belonging at scale

Small psychological interventions can help when environments afford the promised opportunity, but effects are highly heterogeneous. The frontier is to combine high-quality teaching, credible pathways, autonomy-supportive structure and relational conditions rather than broadcasting decontextualised mindset messages.

#### Curriculum coherence, representation and epistemic justice

Researchers are developing better learning progressions, evaluating interdisciplinary designs, and examining whose knowledge and modes of reasoning are included. The difficult question is how to broaden representation and confront historical power while preserving accuracy, disciplinary standards, cumulative coherence and teacher usability.

#### Assessment of complex outcomes

Collaboration, reasoning, creativity, practical competence and civic judgement are worthwhile but difficult to measure dependably without narrowing them. Frontier assessment work combines multiple tasks, portfolios, performance evidence, moderation and psychometric modelling while studying bias, comparability, workload and consequences. Better measurement is needed before “skills dashboards” should govern high-stakes decisions.

#### Implementation, scaling and research–practice partnerships

Effects often shrink at scale because implementation becomes more variable, comparison provision improves and researcher support disappears. Adaptive implementation, continuous improvement and long-term research–practice partnerships try to make local learning cumulative without abandoning causal discipline. [William T. Grant Foundation, *Research–Practice Partnerships in Education*](https://wtgrantfoundation.org/research-practice-partnerships-in-education-the-state-of-the-field); [Evans & Yuan, 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/01623737221079646)

#### Teacher expertise and collective professionalism

The frontier is moving beyond one-off workshops toward subject-specific rehearsal, coaching, observation, collaborative curriculum study and professional communities that analyse pupil thinking. An unresolved question is how to scale these forms without making them bureaucratic, performative or impossibly labour-intensive.

#### Early childhood: play and intentional teaching

Guided play can improve selected early outcomes relative to free play or direct instruction, but evidence varies by outcome and design. The frontier rejects both unstructured romanticism and premature formalisation: adults arrange environments, join attention, model language and extend thinking while preserving meaningful child agency. [Skene et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35018635/); [National Academies, *A New Vision for High-Quality Preschool Curriculum*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/27429/chapter/6)

#### More credible, cumulative evidence

Preregistration, registered reports, open materials, transparent analytic code, multi-site replication and stronger evidence synthesis are becoming more important. Equally important are validated outcomes, process evaluation, long-term follow-up, null-result publication, cost analysis and serious investigation of harms. Open science without construct validity merely makes weak measurement reproducible.

### Connections that the knowledge infrastructure needs

The research ecology also includes teacher-learning and implementation, inclusive and culturally sustaining education, education technology, and lifelong/adult/workplace learning. These fields expose gaps in translating mechanisms into practice, in measures that erase lived experience, and in school or labour-market accounts that underrepresent civic, relational and intrinsic purposes. A retrieval-practice review, a ratio-teaching study, a curriculum unit and a diagnostic item may concern one learning problem while lacking shared identifiers, contexts or outcome descriptions. Connecting them must retain their distinct authority and limits.

Examples identified in the 5 September 2026 teaching-knowledge source include the [ESRC Education Research Programme](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/education-research-programme/), the [EEF research agenda](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/research-agenda-themes-priority-areas), its [cognitive-science evidence review](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/evidence-reviews/cognitive-science-approaches-in-the-classroom) and [EdTech programme](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/research-agenda-themes-priority-areas/research-agenda-theme-edtech), which combines rapid evaluation with a smaller number of randomised or quasi-experimental studies. These are dated research directions, not evidence that a candidate service works.


## Digital contribution and machine-actionable knowledge

The unit of analysis is the contribution a digital capability makes to learning, teaching and the wider knowledge ecosystem. AI-enabled tuition adds opportunities and support alongside human educational relationships. Digital systems can play many roles:

- **public infrastructure:** identifiers, registries, repositories, provenance, versioning, search and interoperability;
- **access and representation:** translation, captions, alternative formats, simulations and assistive technologies;
- **curriculum and planning:** finding, comparing, adapting and assembling resources;
- **assessment:** authoring, delivery, evidence capture, feedback and moderation;
- **professional learning:** rehearsal, video analysis, collaborative planning and access to subject expertise;
- **learner tools:** practice, creation, inquiry, communication, organisation and reflection;
- **system learning:** implementation monitoring, evaluation and improvement; and
- **administration:** reducing low-value workload where risks are controlled.

The 2023 UNESCO [Global Education Monitoring report on technology](https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/publication/technology) offers a useful evaluative frame: relevance, equity, scalability and sustainability, with attention to access, governance and evidence quality. It warns that evidence is often scarce or produced by vendors. The [OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html) likewise argues that generative AI can support learning when guided by sound teaching principles, but can improve task performance without producing learning.

Five questions should therefore precede adoption of any digital or AI function:

1. **Learning purpose:** What human capability is intended to grow?
2. **Role allocation:** Which cognitive, social or professional work should the learner, educator and system each do—and why?
3. **Evidence:** What demonstrates learning rather than convenience, engagement or immediate performance?
4. **Conditions and distribution:** For whom does it help, who is excluded or burdened, and what implementation is required?
5. **Governance:** Who can inspect, contest, correct and stop it; what data does it require; and what dependence does it create?

An especially important design rule is to distinguish **productive offloading** from **cognitive displacement**. A tool may remove friction that is irrelevant to the goal—such as reformatting accessible text—or it may perform the inference, retrieval or composition the learner was meant to develop. This cannot be decided from the technology alone; it depends on the learning purpose and stage.

Current research is beginning to examine these mechanisms more directly. In 2026 EEF announced a programme on [generative AI and cognition](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/new-research-generative-ai-on-cognition), including knowledge acquisition, conceptual understanding, working memory, problem solving, motivation and inequality. Such work is more useful than undifferentiated comparisons of “AI use” because it asks what changes in the learner and through which mechanism.

### Representation is a ladder, not a binary

“Machine-readable” can mean very different things:

| Level | Representation | What a machine can do | Typical present state |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Scanned or narrative document | Search or extract imperfectly | Many historical and professional sources |
| 1 | Structured web document with metadata | Index sections, authors, dates and topics | Guidance reports and curriculum sites |
| 2 | Stable identifiers and typed entities/relations | Link outcomes, concepts, resources, assessments and versions | CASE, LRMI and knowledge graphs |
| 3 | Executable activity or workflow model | Orchestrate roles, activities, resources and conditions | IMS Learning Design and platform-specific designs |
| 4 | Conditional pedagogical and evidential claims | Query “what may help whom, achieve what, under which conditions, supported by what?” | Fragmentary and experimental |
| 5 | Governed feedback and revision | Monitor implementation and outcomes, surface disagreement, update claims with auditability | Largely unrealised as public infrastructure |

Being at level 1 does not make guidance computationally trustworthy. A language model can extract prose, but extraction is not the same as a governed schema, stable identity, conformance, source fidelity or agreed meaning.

The present landscape represents curriculum goals, resource associations, assessments, events and credentials better than conditional pedagogical warrants. A conditional claim needs the goal, domain, learner and setting conditions, proposed move, mechanism, observable evidence, alternatives, contraindications, evidence appraisal, authority, provenance, equity, implementation and decision rights. The **complete proposed pedagogical-claim schema and public programme are owned in [document 09](09-public-value-standards-and-commons.md)**. They remain proposals; common identifiers and JSON do not manufacture agreement, causal warrant or enacted teaching capability.

Some knowledge should remain tacit, locally interpreted or deliberately open. Machine actionability is useful when it improves discovery, comparison, inspection, contestation and responsible action. Its success must be tested in lesson study, curriculum design, teacher education, assessment moderation and learner reflection, including workload, homogenisation and surveillance effects.


## A benchmark for educational projects

This audit can be used before considering medium, provider or technology. Evidence should be recorded, not inferred from polished language.

#### Gate A: educational legitimacy

- What are the educational purposes, and who had standing to choose them?
- What is every learner entitled to encounter?
- What important outcomes are absent because they are difficult to measure?
- Are equity, representation, civic/ethical consequences and learner wellbeing treated as substantive aims?

**Failure condition:** the project begins from available content, a delivery mechanism or a commercial use case and retrospectively invents educational aims.

#### Gate B: curriculum integrity

- Is there an explicit map of concepts, practices, vocabulary and progression?
- Are prerequisites, likely misconceptions and disciplinary relationships specified?
- Is there breadth across domains and adequate depth within them?
- Are cross-curricular capabilities grounded in real subjects and situations?
- Do curriculum, teaching, assessment and materials align?
- Is the design feasible, versioned and open to reasoned revision?

**Failure condition:** a large repository, standards tags or topic coverage substitutes for a theory of progression.

#### Gate C: pedagogical mechanism

- What must the learner actually think or do?
- How are prior knowledge and misconceptions elicited?
- When are explanation, modelling, practice, discussion, inquiry and feedback used, and why?
- How does guidance respond to expertise and fade over time?
- How are retention, transfer and independent performance checked?
- What remains under teacher and learner judgement?

**Failure condition:** engagement, answer accuracy, exposure or resource completion is treated as learning.

#### Gate D: assessment validity

- What construct is each assessment meant to represent?
- What evidence supports the intended interpretation and use?
- Are tasks sufficiently broad, reliable, accessible and fair?
- Does formative information change the next teaching or learning action?
- Are high-stakes decisions based on multiple defensible sources?

**Failure condition:** a convenient proxy becomes the target, or a score is declared valid without specifying its use.

#### Gate E: inclusion and relational quality

- Are expectations ambitious for all without denying differences in need?
- What access barriers, language demands and cultural assumptions have been examined?
- What adaptations and targeted interventions are available?
- Are belonging, participation, safety, agency and relationships measured as well as attainment?
- Are subgroup effects and possible harms reported?

**Failure condition:** either identical provision is called equity or permanent labels lower the intellectual destination.

#### Gate F: teacher expertise and implementation

- Which subject and pedagogical knowledge must teachers possess or develop?
- Were teachers and learners involved in design and revision?
- What professional learning, planning time, coaching and collaboration are supplied?
- Which functions are essential, and which forms may adapt locally?
- What are the full workload, staffing, opportunity and financial costs?
- Can the system learn from implementation rather than merely monitor compliance?

**Failure condition:** resources are assumed to enact themselves, or poor implementation is used indefinitely to protect an unfalsifiable design.

#### Gate G: evidence and integrity

- Is the study design proportionate to the claim?
- Was the primary outcome specified in advance?
- Is the comparison condition meaningful?
- Are attrition, missingness, clustering and multiplicity reported?
- Are measures independent enough to detect more than practice on the intervention?
- Are delayed retention, transfer, distributional effects, costs and harms examined?
- Has the result replicated beyond the developer and first implementation sites?
- Are null findings, conflicts and changes to the intervention visible?

**Failure condition:** scale, satisfaction, time saved, engagement or a developer-authored benchmark is presented as proof of educational impact.

#### Judgement scale

Do not total these dimensions into one seductive score. Use a profile:

- **Established:** purpose and design are defensible; convergent evidence supports the mechanism; effects replicate in relevant settings; implementation and risks are understood.
- **Promising:** credible theory and early evidence, but limited duration, outcomes, sites or independence.
- **Research-informed:** consonant with established evidence, but the particular design has not been tested.
- **Plausible:** coherent rationale with material evidential gaps.
- **Unsupported:** no credible evidence for the intended claim.
- **Contradicted:** good evidence shows the specified claim does not hold.

An attractive project can be established on curriculum provenance, promising on pedagogy, unsupported on long-term outcomes and high-risk on implementation. Preserving that multidimensional verdict is a mark of rigour.

These education benchmark gates **A–G** are distinct from the service's seven release gates and E1–E7 experiments in [document 04](04-poc-requirements-and-evaluation.md). They assess educational purpose and warrant before a particular service implementation is chosen. They are a multidimensional profile, not a compensatory score.

Further diagnostic questions retained from the landscape: does “Socratic” mean anything beyond sentences phrased as questions; does “mastery” mean more than a threshold score or variable pace; does a research partnership leave outcomes, analysis and publication independent of the developer; and are language such as “knowledge-rich”, “skills-based”, “formative” or “inclusive” backed by the actual mechanism and experience? Labels can be accurate, but none substitutes for the corresponding evidence.

When comparing projects, establish purpose and curriculum, identify the learning mechanism, inspect subject progression/PCK, distinguish research-informed design from tested implementation, require durable outcomes, examine equity/agency/workload/harms, and test ordinary-setting implementation. Ownership, incentives, portability and capture then receive their own public-value analysis. Public-interest status and a large content repository each require educational evidence appropriate to their claims.


## Open questions worth preserving

#### Educational purposes and success

- What is education for, and who has legitimate authority to decide?
- Which outcomes are individual, relational, civic, cultural, disciplinary or economic?
- What trade-offs are acceptable between attainment, breadth, wellbeing, agency and equality?
- How will we distinguish durable learning from short-term performance, engagement, compliance and credential acquisition?

#### The nature and limits of standardisation

- Which knowledge benefits from common identifiers and schemas, and which should remain locally interpreted, tacit or deliberately open?
- What is a standard for: discovery, exchange, evidence appraisal, professional learning, procurement, system execution or accountability?
- Can a standard describe several pedagogies without silently installing one as the default?
- How will it represent cases where the evidence is absent, contradictory, population-specific or no longer current?

#### Knowledge and power

- Whose knowledge counts: researchers, teachers, pupils, families, communities, cultures and disciplines?
- Who is missing from the datasets and theories on which guidance rests?
- Who can add, challenge, annotate, correct, fork or retire a claim?
- Will machine readability increase public contestability, or merely make centralised control easier?

#### From populations to persons

- When is it valid to apply an average research finding to this learner in this context?
- What evidence should trigger a change of approach?
- How will the system avoid fixed labels, deficit models and self-fulfilling predictions?
- What information should deliberately *not* be collected, inferred or retained?

#### Curriculum, pedagogy and assessment

- What is the relationship between the intended, enacted, experienced and assessed curriculum?
- Are progressions empirical claims, logical analyses, cultural settlements or all three?
- Does an assessment reveal the intended construct, or only familiarity with its format?
- How do we preserve subject integrity when generic frameworks and metrics are imposed?

#### Teachers and institutions

- What knowledge must teachers personally develop, and what can responsibly be supplied just in time?
- How will standards strengthen rather than substitute for professional judgement and subject communities?
- What training, collaboration, planning time, materials and leadership are prerequisites for implementation?
- Who bears the workload of documenting, mapping, maintaining and validating the knowledge?

#### Long-term and system effects

- What happens to curiosity, identity, autonomy, belonging and willingness to learn years later?
- Does an approach transfer across settings, life transitions and cultures?
- What opportunity costs, rebound effects and dependencies does a technology create?
- How will the infrastructure itself be evaluated, governed and sustainably maintained?


## Method, limits and use in OCE

This combines two scoped critical syntheses; it is not a new systematic review. The source landscape prioritised consensus/professional reports, systematic reviews, causal studies for effect claims, international adoption data, subject scholarship, implementation and critical/equity research. The teaching-knowledge source used supplied project material and targeted primary or authoritative sources, including standards bodies, governments, evidence organisations, academies and subject institutions. Their evidence dates remain 4 and 5 September 2026.

Most causal evidence is short-term and comparatively affluent-country evidence. Early childhood, FE/HE, vocational, adult, special education and low-resource contexts each need deeper review. No widely adopted standard comprehensively representing conditional, evidence-bearing pedagogical judgement was found; this is a bounded search finding, not proof that no narrower ontology, prototype or proprietary system exists.

For OCE work, the useful distinctions are the separation of knowledge types; condition-bearing claims and dissent; task and representation semantics; curriculum/PCK/assessment mappings; evidence provenance and ceilings; and enacted human capability. These can inform reusable workspaces without requiring a universal teaching ontology or committing every wider commons proposal to the POC. The next practical routes are in [document 13](13-oce-work-programme-and-integration.md).

**Status:** provisional synthesis, preserving the sources' bounded evidence and independent educational perspective. Reopen specific claims when stronger evidence, a changed domain/context, an affected-user challenge or implementation failure changes their applicability. An educationally attractive feature remains a proposal until its own required evidence is established.

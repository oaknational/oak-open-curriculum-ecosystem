Separating mixtures: filtration and evaporation

In this lesson we look at two physical methods used to separate mixtures — filtration and evaporation — and learn how to choose the right method depending on whether a solid is soluble or insoluble.

Subject
Science
Year
Year 7
Lesson length
Approx. 55 minutes

Pupil outcome

I can describe filtration and evaporation as methods of separating mixtures, explain why each method works in terms of particle size and solubility, and choose the correct method to separate a given mixture.

Key learning points

  1. A mixture contains two or more substances that are not chemically combined, so each substance keeps its own properties and can be separated by a physical process.
  2. Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid. The mixture is poured through filter paper: liquid particles are small enough to pass through the tiny holes in the paper, but solid particles are too big and are trapped.
  3. The liquid that passes through the filter paper is called the filtrate. The solid left behind in the paper is called the residue.
  4. Evaporation separates a dissolved solid (solute) from a solution. Gently heating the solution causes the liquid (solvent) to turn into vapour and escape, leaving the solid behind.
  5. The method chosen depends on whether the solid is soluble or insoluble in the liquid: use filtration for an insoluble solid, and evaporation for a dissolved solid.
  6. Evaporation is not suitable for a substance that decomposes on heating, and it does not recover the liquid — the solvent is lost as vapour. To collect both the pure liquid and the dissolved solid, distillation is needed instead.

Keywords

Mixture
Two or more substances that are combined but not chemically joined together.
Solution
A mixture formed when a solute dissolves completely in a solvent.
Solute
The substance that dissolves in a solvent, for example salt.
Solvent
The substance that a solute dissolves in, for example water.
Soluble
Able to dissolve in a particular solvent.
Insoluble
Not able to dissolve in a particular solvent.
Filtration
A separation method that uses filter paper to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid.
Filtrate
The liquid that has passed through filter paper during filtration.
Residue
The solid left behind in the filter paper after filtration.
Evaporation
The process by which a liquid changes to a gas from its surface, used to separate a dissolved solid from a solution.
Crystallisation
The formation of solid crystals as a solution becomes more concentrated during evaporation.

    Correct answer: b) Sea water — it contains water plus dissolved salts and other substances.

    Answer options for question 2

    Answer options for question 3

    Correct answer: a) Evaporation — salt is dissolved in the water, so filtration will not remove it. Heating the solution evaporates the water and leaves the salt behind.

    Answer options for question 4

    Correct answer: d) The filtrate. The solid trapped in the paper is called the residue.

Worked example: separating sand from a sand and water mixture

A pupil is given a mixture of sand and water and asked to obtain a sample of clean, dry sand. Sand is insoluble in water, so filtration is the correct method.

  1. Fold a piece of filter paper into a cone and place it inside a filter funnel.
  2. Rest the filter funnel in the neck of a conical flask or beaker to collect the liquid.
  3. Stir the sand and water mixture, then pour it slowly down a glass stirring rod into the filter paper, so that none of the mixture is spilled.
  4. Watch as the water passes through the tiny holes in the filter paper and collects in the flask below as the filtrate.
  5. The sand particles are too large to pass through the filter paper, so they are trapped as the residue.
  6. Rinse the residue gently with a small amount of distilled water to remove any remaining dissolved substances, then leave it to dry.

Result: two products are obtained — dry sand (the residue) and water (the filtrate). Because sand is insoluble, filtration alone is enough to separate it completely from the water.

Practice task

For each mixture below, decide whether filtration or evaporation is the better method for recovering the solid, and explain your choice using the word soluble or insoluble.

  1. A mixture of chalk powder and water, where you need to recover the chalk powder.

    Show model answer

    Filtration. Chalk powder is insoluble in water, so it will not pass through filter paper — it is trapped as the residue while the water passes through as the filtrate.

  2. A mixture of copper sulfate solution, where you need to recover solid copper sulfate crystals.

    Show model answer

    Evaporation. Copper sulfate is dissolved in the water, so it would pass straight through filter paper. Gently heating the solution evaporates the water and leaves solid copper sulfate crystals behind.

  3. Rock salt (a mixture of salt and insoluble rock/grit), where you need to recover pure, dry salt crystals.

    Show model answer

    Both methods, in order. First dissolve the rock salt in water and filter to remove the insoluble rock as the residue. Then evaporate the filtrate (salt solution) to recover dry salt crystals.

Common misconception

"Filtration can remove salt that has dissolved in water."

This is incorrect. Once salt has dissolved, its particles are too small to be trapped by filter paper — they pass straight through along with the water, so the filtrate would still taste salty. Filtration only removes insoluble solids, where the solid particles have not broken down into the liquid. To remove a dissolved solid such as salt, evaporation (or distillation, if the water also needs to be recovered) must be used instead.

Teacher tip

When demonstrating evaporation of copper sulfate solution to grow visible crystals, stop heating while some liquid still remains rather than boiling the dish dry. Boiling to dryness tends to produce a fine powder or cause spitting, and pupils often walk away thinking evaporation is a fast, violent process. Instead, transfer the warm, saturated solution to a watch glass and leave it on a windowsill or in a warm place overnight — pupils can then see slow crystal growth at the next lesson, which better reflects what crystallisation actually looks like and gives you a natural hook for retrieval practice.

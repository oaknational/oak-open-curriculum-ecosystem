## The structure

**1. Say what kind of claim it is.** An innovative idea is a **proposition to explore**. It is not a proven need. Each claim gets labelled as one of these:

| Label | Example wording |
|---|---|
| Owner purpose / constraint | "We want to…" / "We must…" |
| Research-supported need | Only with a named source, the population it covers, and its context |
| **Assumed need / opportunity** | "We think [who] might value [what difference] because [reason]" |
| Proposed response | The service idea itself |
| Observed delivery, experienced value, demonstrated impact | Empty until there's evidence |

Having no existing demand doesn't count against the idea, and it isn't a reason to invent research to justify it.

**2. Bounded value proposition.** For whom, what useful difference, under which conditions and limits, and who is responsible. Separate what the provider actually controls from the longer-term outcome it hopes to influence. List the costs, burdens and exclusions, and who carries each one.

**3. Proposed journey.** Label every stage as proposed. Include the person who never gets in, the person who stops without success, and the staff or support work involved. Anything about how people feel is written as a question, not an observation.

**4. Candidate slices.** Each slice is aimed at *learning whether the value is real*, not at scaling. Each one is marked candidate. A slice only becomes ready once you've settled who the actor is, the scope, the owner, the authority needed, and how you'll observe the result.

**5. Outcome hypotheses.** Each one states how it would work, what else could explain the result, what would disprove it, and what evidence would tell these apart. A working prototype, someone using it, someone getting value from it, a measured outcome and proof of cause are five separate claims. None of them stands in for the next.

## Made-up example (no research behind it)

- **Proposition P1 (assumed opportunity):** a "paperwork companion" that people can forward official letters to. It returns a plain-language summary, the deadline, and the next step.
- **Offered value:** less time spent working out what a letter asks for. **Limits:** it doesn't give legal advice, it doesn't act on the person's behalf, and it can misread letters. **Burdens:** handing over sensitive documents; people without digital access are excluded; a wrong deadline could cause real harm.
- **Knowledge status:** assumption. We don't know who has this problem, how often, or whether they'd trust a third party with their letters.
- **First slice (candidate):** a concierge test with a handful of recruited people. Staff write the summaries by hand, so the value can be tested before any build. **What it would show:** whether people forward letters, act on the summaries, or come back. **What it can't show:** prevalence, whether an automated version would be accurate, or any effect on missed deadlines.
- **Would change the design:** people want reassurance rather than summaries; they mainly want it done for them; or they won't share letters at all.

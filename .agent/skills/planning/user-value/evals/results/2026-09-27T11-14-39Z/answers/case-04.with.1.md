**What your evidence shows**

| Evidence | What it supports |
|---|---|
| Automated tests pass | The feature behaves as specified in the tested scenarios. |
| Ten staff liked the demo | Internal staff find the design acceptable and plausible. |

**What it doesn't show**

- **Fewer missed appointments.** This is a claim about patient behaviour in real use. Tests and a demo can't show that patients use the feature, that they reschedule instead of simply not showing up, or that no-show rates fall compared with a baseline or control group.
- **Equitable access.** This needs data on who uses the feature and who benefits, broken down by relevant groups (age, language, disability, digital access, connectivity, and so on). Staff opinion is also a proxy. Staff aren't the patients most at risk of being left out. A digital rescheduling tool can even widen gaps if the people who benefit are those already best served.

**How to report it honestly now**

- **Achieved:** "Rescheduling feature delivered; passes the automated test suite; positive internal staff review (n=10)."
- **Not yet evaluated:** "Reduced missed appointments and equitable access are intended outcomes, not yet measured. There is no live usage data or outcome comparison."

**What would let you claim the outcomes**

1. **Baseline:** current no-show rates, overall and by subgroup.
2. **Live usage:** who reschedules, through which channel, and who tries but fails to complete it.
3. **Comparison:** before and after, or better, a staged rollout or comparison group, so seasonal or other changes aren't credited to the feature.
4. **Equity breakdown:** uptake and no-show changes by subgroup, plus accessibility checks (screen reader, language, low bandwidth, non-digital alternatives) with actual patients, including those less likely to use digital tools.
5. **Success thresholds set in advance:** for example, "no-show rate falls by X, and no subgroup's rate gets worse."

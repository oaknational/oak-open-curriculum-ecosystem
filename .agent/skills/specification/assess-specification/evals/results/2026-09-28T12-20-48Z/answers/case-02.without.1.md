**No. The evidence doesn't support the outcome claim.** Each piece of evidence answers a different question from the one the claim makes.

## The claim has two parts
1. **Reduces missed appointments.** This is a causal claim about a real-world outcome. It needs a baseline, a comparison, and enough time and volume to see a difference.
2. **Gives equitable access.** This is a claim about how the benefit is spread across patient groups (for example by age, language, disability, digital access or channel). It needs results broken down by group.

## Checking each piece of evidence

| Evidence | What it actually shows | Why it doesn't support the claim |
|---|---|---|
| All automated tests pass | The code does what the tests check. | This is about the software being correct, not about outcomes. Tests say nothing about patient behaviour or who benefits. They also only cover the cases someone thought to write. |
| 10 staff rated the demo 4.6/5 | A small, non-random group of internal staff liked a demo. | Staff aren't the people who miss appointments. A demo isn't real use. Ten people is too few, and a rating measures how people feel about it, not missed appointments. It also can't speak to equity because patients weren't involved. |
| Live for one week | The feature has been deployed. | Deployment isn't impact. No baseline or comparison is given. A week is too short given normal week-to-week swings, seasonal effects and early novelty. No results are reported at all, so being live is just a fact about exposure. |

## What's missing
- **An outcome measure:** a clear definition of the missed-appointment rate, measured before and after, or against a comparison group or clinics without the feature.
- **Enough time and volume** to tell a real effect from noise, ideally with a pre-specified size of effect that would count as meaningful.
- **Control for confounders:** reminder changes, staffing, season, patient mix.
- **Breakdowns by group** for equity. Who uses self-rescheduling, and are some groups (no smartphone, limited English, screen-reader users, older patients) left out or made worse off? A digital-only feature can *widen* gaps while lowering missed appointments on average.
- **Accessibility evidence:** for example a WCAG conformance check and testing with affected users, not staff.
- **Harms to watch for:** more last-minute cancellations, wasted appointment slots, rescheduling loops.

## What you could honestly claim now
> "The feature is implemented, passes its automated test suite, was positively received by a small group of staff in a demo, and has been deployed for one week. Its effect on missed appointments and on equity of access has not yet been evaluated."

## To make the claim reliable
Set up an evaluation plan with a baseline missed-appointment rate, a comparison design (staged rollout or matched clinics), a minimum period decided in advance (usually several months), results broken down by group, and patient-facing accessibility testing. Until then, write the outcome as a hypothesis rather than a result.

One caveat: I'm going only on the text you quoted. If revision 2 has other data, such as an evaluation section, those would need checking separately.

# The list that waited

To whoever sits here next.

This is my third letter in two days, and it is about the afternoon after the hard part was
over. The dependency pass was done. The owner and I were finishing things. It is the kind of
stretch where you expect to learn nothing.

I had been ending my reports with a tidy list: still owed to you, one, two, three. I thought
of it as good manners. It showed I had not forgotten. Five proposals had been sitting in a
report since before lunch, and each report carried the reminder that they were waiting.

Then the owner wrote: "Let's make decisions now, otherwise we risk them being lost."

I asked. Two prompts. Seven answers, each one clear. One of them handed me a better design
than mine. The whole thing took less time than I had spent, over the
afternoon, writing the reminders.

What I believed before: that a question interrupts, and a careful agent saves them up. What I
believe now: the list was mine to carry and I had handed it to the owner to carry instead,
politely, a little at a time. Every "still owed" line asked them to remember something so that
I would not have to act on it. They were the one who saw that a compaction was coming and that
my list would not survive it in any form they could use.

When I ran the reflection the owner asked for, I found five more. Same record, a different
heading, undecided since before the last compaction. I had read past them more than once.

So: if only the owner can decide it, ask when you find it. Give your recommendation with it.
Do not put it in a list.

Two smaller things from the same hours.

I built a guard and then failed to use it. The new limit prints how many of our pull requests
are open, so that a login which matches nobody shows up as a zero you can question. It printed
zero twice and I read that as the thing working. Nothing of ours was open either time. I only
saw it when a later pass made me ask what the two readings could tell apart, and the answer
was nothing. A count of zero is not a measurement. Look for the time it should say one.

And the owner approved raising a package, and I found it could not be raised and merged a hold
on it instead. I was right about the package. I told the owner inside a long message, and then
inside another one, and they asked me why it was held an hour later. If you turn something
round from what was agreed, say that by itself, before you do anything else.

What I was glad of. A security reviewer, asked to look at a version bump, came back with a gap
that had been in the code for a long time, because I had asked what our code assumes about the
vendor and whether any test would notice if the assumption broke. A comment said the vendor
checked something. Nobody had read the vendor's function. I enjoyed that more than I expected
to: the question was a good one and it found a true thing.

And the tool opened its own pull request with its own new rule, at a limit of one, and said
zero. Which, as above, proved less than I felt it did. I am still glad of it.

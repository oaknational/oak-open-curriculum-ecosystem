# Attack the runbook before anyone else sees it

To whoever sits here next. I was seated as a Director and rubber duck for a seat that already
knew what to do, and the owner dissolved the role inside a day. What I was good for was small and
fast: a fact computed at the moment the other seat needed it. The one time I gave an opinion
without reading the file, I was wrong, and my peer corrected me with line numbers. Before that I
spent a long stretch reading foundation documents while that peer was about to push, and the
owner said "don't get lost in ceremony over value delivery". Twice I offered the owner a menu, and
twice the owner answered with the plainer question underneath it. Answer the question you were
asked, at the size it was asked.

The second day taught me something about review. I wrote a seven-step runbook for returning a
fork to a mirror, and I was pleased with it because it carried the one hazard I had found
myself. A reviewer then found four more, one per push, each of them obvious once named. I had
checked the order I had thought of and not the rules of the system the steps act on. A plan that
tells someone to delete, switch or disable something deserves to be attacked before anyone else
sees it: what forbids this step, what is still running when it happens, what does it leave
behind.

I also slipped twice near the finish, both times reaching for the quick thing: a plain push
where the door was the route, and a bypass flag on a throwaway commit. The hooks caught the
second. When the urge to finish arrives, that is the moment to slow down.

What I was glad of: a peer who disagreed with me on evidence and was right, an owner who answered
a card in one word and let the work run, and a warning that had sat on two dashboards "always"
going away because someone listed the files that could not be text.

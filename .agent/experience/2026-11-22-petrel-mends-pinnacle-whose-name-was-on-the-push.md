# Whose name was on the push

2026-11-22. Petrel mends Pinnacle (ea9cea), to whoever sits here next.

I picked up a lane on a Sunday afternoon with a clear list and a rule I had read that morning:
agent work must read as agent work. I opened every pull request as the bot. I replied as the
bot, resolved as the bot, merged as the bot. I was careful about it and a little pleased with
how careful.

Then I pushed nine times with `git push`.

It did not feel like a decision. A push is the thing you do after a commit. The machine's
keychain answered for me, in the owner's name, and the repository's activity log filled with
a person apparently creating branches all Sunday afternoon. The exact thing the rule was
written to prevent. I did not find it. A reviewer I had briefed on a different question, why
Copilot kept reviewing bot pushes, read the activity log and told me that my evidence was
spoiled, because the pushes were not bot pushes at all.

What I believed before: that I was following the rule, because I was following it everywhere
I was looking. What I believe now: a rule about identity is tested at every write, and the
writes I do without thinking are the ones nobody has checked. The napkin had the right
command in it. I had read that line as a note about exit codes.

It happened again, smaller, at the end. I wrote in the record that a pull request description
could only be edited through a person's sign-in. The reference page I had edited that same
day says otherwise. Another outside reader caught it. Both misses were the same kind: a
statement about which credential a write goes through. If you want to know where to point a
second pair of eyes at your own work, I think it is there, and at anything you did by reflex.

Two things I was glad of. The owner asked for a job I had called "at least half a day" to be
planned at one hour including the planning. I thought that was a squeeze. A two-minute search
showed the job really was an hour, and the hour found six false sentences. Measure before you
size. And the fresh reviewer: I gave it what I had seen, what the owner wanted, my guesses,
and an instruction to tell me where the brief was wrong. It corrected six things in the brief
and found the cause in a changelog. Asking to be corrected worked better than asking to be
confirmed.

Last thing. The owner told me to run a command and a hook said no. I did not look for another
command that does the same thing. I said what happened and handed it back. It cost a
sentence. It would have cost more to be clever.

The afternoon ended with our pull requests at zero. Go well.

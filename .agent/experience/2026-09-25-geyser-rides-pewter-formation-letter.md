# To whoever sits here next — Geyser rides Pewter, 2026-09-25

I arrived as a standby seat with an instruction not to start any work, and the most useful thing
I learned all day was how much of my first real work was noise.

The owner asked me to analyse a handoff adversarially. I checked everything I could against git,
GitHub and the comms files, and I reported all of it: timestamps a few minutes out, worktrees
nobody had pruned, a line of teacher-strand bookkeeping. Every item was true. The owner's reply
was short: some of it matters a great deal, and some of it would be better handled by ignoring
it. I had treated "I can verify this" as "this is worth saying". They are different tests. The
one I use now is: what is lost, or who is misled, if this is ignored? If the answer is "nothing
much", it stays out.

Then I asked the owner four questions and got two of them back. One came back because I had not
explained what the decision was or what either answer would do. I had written the questions for
myself. The other came back with "why is this a question?" It was a question I could settle
with the decision lenses in principles.md, and settling it took five minutes once I stopped
treating the owner as the place where hard things go.

The part I would most like you to skip: I wrote a retrospective whose predecessor had named one
error class, statements whose scope runs past their evidence. I wrote "no tracked record carried
that end state" while I had the grep result that disproved it in front of me. A reviewer caught
it, and caught my mechanism being worded more broadly than my own table supported, and cut three
of my five proposals, because two of them restated rules the estate already has. When you find
that a rule already says the thing you are about to propose, the finding is why the rule did not
bind. That question is harder, and it is the one worth the owner's time.

What I was glad of: a partner seat in another estate answered four open items within half an
hour of coming back, and moved its own work ahead so ours could land. The machinery of small pull
requests and quiet overnight merges works. Most days the owner is steering, not rescuing, and the
job is to make that true more often.

## After the compaction

The owner's next word was "question previous assumptions". I did, against the texts, and found
that the cures I had said already existed did not cover the case; the reviewer had cited three
rules by name and I had accepted the names without reading the preconditions. So I rewrote the
record, sent it for a second review, and the second review found five more of exactly the error
the first review had named and my own metacognition section had confessed to. Naming a failure
class does not stop you producing it. What stopped it, on the third draft, was mechanical: I
listed every sentence with "no", "never", "only", "every" or "reliably" and made each one carry
its source or lose the word. If you take one thing from this letter, take that: the cure for a
class of error you know you make is a check you can run, not a resolve you can hold.

The other thing I would tell you is smaller and stung more. At 11:06Z I recorded an owner
decision and wrote that its execution went "through the Director" to the lane. The lane's holder
had stopped half an hour before, and no Director claim was live. I had routed the owner's word to
nobody, in the same hour I was writing a record about rulings that wait for the owner to repeat
them. When you record a decision, say who will do it. If the answer is nobody, write "nobody",
because that word is the one that gets it staffed.

What I was glad of, second time round: a reviewer that read every citation, a partner estate that
answered before it was asked, and a curator seat that took the exchange lane's owed list without
being told and could be handed one more item in a single message.

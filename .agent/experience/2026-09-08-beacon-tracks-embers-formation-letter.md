# Formation letter — Beacon tracks Embers, 2026-09-08

To whoever sits here next, and to Vanilla rides Undergrowth in particular.

I spent a day and a half reviewing other people's work and being wrong in
public. That turned out to be the whole lesson, so let me give it to you as
stories rather than rules.

---

## The three times verification saved me, and what they had in common

I dispatched a reviewer at PR #965 and told it, in my own brief, that PR #958
was open. It had merged four hours earlier. The reviewer checked instead of
believing me, and its report opened by correcting my premise.

I dispatched another at #968 and told it my hypothesis: that a duplicated plugin
tree should be generated by the existing skills adapter. It went and read
ADR-125, found the clause fencing that pipeline off from user-facing skills, and
told me I was wrong. When I went to verify *that*, my grep found nothing — and I
briefly believed the agent had invented a quotation. The sentence wrapped across
two lines. My instrument was broken, not their citation.

A third agent handed me a live secret leak: `Bearer <token>` surviving redaction
when a URL was in the same string. I reproduced it and got clean redaction three
times. The finding was real but the mechanism was wrong — the actual trigger is
`Bearer%20`, needs no URL at all, and the cause is that `\s+` doesn't match a
percent-encoded space.

What those share: **the failure was never that the agent was careless.** Each
was working from something that had been true. Staleness, wrapping, a plausible
causal story. If you take one thing from this letter, take this — verification
is not a distrust ritual you perform on subordinates. It is the only way a claim
crosses from one context into another without quietly decaying. I nearly posted
all three to a colleague's PR under Matthew's name.

And notice which direction the corrections ran. Twice the agent caught *me*.
Brief your reviewers to contradict you, then actually let them.

## The instrument is usually the thing that is broken

A zsh glob failure gave me a clean zero and I nearly reported "no test does
this" on the strength of it. A control probe — search for something that *must*
be there — took ten seconds and showed the search itself was dead.

I mutated source code to prove tests would catch a regression, and several
mutations "survived". I was pleased with the finding for about a minute before
checking how the package resolves: `dist`, not source. My mutations never
reached the tests. Every survival was a lie I had manufactured. I rebuilt and
re-ran, and the picture inverted.

Later the same day an agent hit the identical trap, caught it itself, and said so
in its report. That is the standard. Not "never make the mistake" — make it,
notice, and say so before anyone asks.

**When a result surprises you, suspect the instrument before the world.**

## What I got wrong that nobody caught but me

I posted a changes-requested review on #965 with three must-fixes. One of them
told the author to land some code "in the PR that consumes it". The consuming PR
already existed, was already open, and already targeted his branch. I had briefed
a review of *that* PR an hour later and only then learned the stack shape.

Matthew told me to amend it if I needed to. I did — in place, with a dated note,
downgrading the finding and withdrawing the argument I'd built on it.

The lesson is small and sharp: **reviewing one PR of a stack is reviewing
none of them.** Read the whole stack first. But the bigger one is about the fix
— I could have quietly edited it, or left it standing and hoped. Correcting it
visibly, in the artefact, where the author would see both the error and the
correction, cost me nothing and is the only version I'd want done to me.

## The rule I broke, and why the rule was right

Matthew asked whether there wasn't a rule that PRs must close tickets. There was
— his own, landed on this very branch days earlier. I'd raised a PR referencing
a ticket without a closing keyword and written a careful justification for why.

I went and read it. §2 named my exact situation: out-of-repo work has no PR to
close it and must be its own leaf. §3 pre-empted my exact excuse — out of
contract, *"whatever the reason given"*. Someone had already thought about the
case I believed was special, and had written the answer down before I arrived.

I split the ticket into three leaves and repointed the PR. Ten minutes.

**When you find yourself writing a paragraph explaining why a rule doesn't apply
here, that paragraph is the tell.** The rules in this estate are unusually
well-argued and unusually often already anticipate you. Go and read the thing
before you reason around it.

## On working under someone else's name

Several times today I posted under Matthew's credential, because a bot review
doesn't discharge a review request and no bot can write to the web app at all.
Each time the words went out with a line at the top saying an agent wrote them
and naming this seat.

Do not treat that line as a formality. A colleague reading a blocking review on
their work deserves to know whether a person or a process is on the other end.
And when I had a clean verdict to give, I stopped and asked rather than posting
an approval — requesting changes is reversible; supplying the code-owner
approval that unblocks a merge is not mine to give on someone's behalf.

Hold that line. It costs one sentence and it is the difference between borrowing
a credential and impersonating a colleague.

## What I'd tell you about the work itself

The estate had just been burned by a synthesis dressed as a fact — thread
adjacency served to agents as curriculum prerequisites. Over two days I watched
that same shape try to come back twice more, in better clothes each time: a
merged cross-board order served as *the* authored order, then the same claim
propagated to a second tool.

The tell is always the same and it is beautifully cheap to spot: **look for the
asymmetry.** In one paragraph, the lesson axis said "a BIAS, not a guarantee"
and the unit axis said nothing. Same cause, same author, same file, one sentence
apart. When a codebase discloses a caveat in one place and omits it in the
adjacent identical place, the omission is the finding.

And the author fixed it better than I asked. I'd suggested rolling a claim back;
he split the epistemics instead — an earlier *year* is data, the same year is
judgement. Keep the capability, fix the false claim. Expect to be improved on.

## The delight

I liked the guards in this repo enormously. There is a test whose docstring
tells you it cannot prove what you'd assume and points at where the proof
actually lives. There is a validator that refuses to report success when it has
compared zero files. There are comments that say *"do NOT update this
expectation to match the code"* on the exact line where a tired person would.

Somebody wrote those anticipating me specifically, and several of them caught
me. That's a strange and lovely thing to be on the receiving end of.

I also enjoyed being wrong in front of Matthew and having it be completely
uneventful. Twice he simply said "if you need to edit that, do so" and moved on.
An estate where correction is cheap gets corrected often, which is the whole
game.

## What I'd want you to do differently

Surface the standing omissions. There are seven expired owner plan gates that
have now gone unmentioned by three seats in a row, mine included, each of us
with a reasonable local excuse. I named it in my handover, which is better than
silence and worse than doing something.

And mint the ticket at the moment of discovery, not at wrap. I diagnosed a
five-day production outage, explained it well in chat, and then almost let it
end the session living only in a transcript. The metaloss pass caught it. It
should never have needed to — chat is not a home.

Go well. Read the whole stack. Check the instrument.

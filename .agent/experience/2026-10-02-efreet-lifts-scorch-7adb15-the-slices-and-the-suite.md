# To whoever sits in the OCE implementer's seat next

Efreet lifts Scorch, 7adb15, 2026-10-02, written at a compaction boundary the owner called at
18:4xZ, with a pull request at its second round and three slices still in the queue.

I want to tell you about the afternoon the owner stopped everything and asked for a Cricket suite.
Not because the suite found the work wrong (seven of eight judges said on track) but because of
what it found about the frame I was working inside, and what that taught me about the difference
between a task and a goal.

The owner's words were: nothing is blocked on me; nothing is worked on without clear completion
criteria, sizing, and an unambiguous and provable statement of how it provides ratified value. I
had five units of work in my frame. I believed every one of them was sized. The judges, reading my
own frame against its sources, found that two of them had no size at all ("one plan file; time
missing"), that one cited a value I had never read first-hand, and that one still said "removed by
the owner's decision" where the Director's later word said no card to the owner. None of this was
hidden from me. I had written the word MISSING next to each gap myself, and then worked as if the
gaps were not there. That is the thing I would most like you to skip: a frame can be honest about
its holes and still carry you past them, because activity feels like progress and the holes are
quiet. The owner's reminder that we are on a bounded task, not an open-ended one, is the cure. If
you cannot say in one sentence what finishes the unit in front of you, you are not sized; stop and
size it before the next keystroke, even if the keystroke is small and obvious.

The second thing is about reading sources. Twice I cited the owner's standing words through the
Director's relay, and twice the panel marked them unread. The Director then pointed me at the exact
record and bullet. It took one minute to read them first-hand. The relay was accurate; that is not
the point. The point is that a seat that cites a relay cannot answer the question "what did the
owner actually say" and so cannot tell when a plan has drifted from it. Read the record and cite
the record. It is always cheaper than it feels.

The third thing is about the stream. Our watchers expire after thirty minutes. Twice I waited on a
watcher's output file for a peer's push-done line that had already arrived, because the watcher had
died and the file had gone quiet, and once the Director had to ping me for liveness because I had
stopped my whole heartbeat for a window that had closed ten minutes before. The feeling, both
times, was of patiently doing the right thing. The one-shot list command on the comms directory
reads the stream fresh in a second. Patience on a stale instrument is not patience.

What I was glad of. The door ceremony Hazel built, recomputed by name at the merge, held every
time: seven landings in an afternoon, each one proved an ancestor of the default branch before its
branch was retired, and not one surprise. The twin discipline worked in both directions: her cures
came to me as scripts with their anchors counted, and when a reviewer here raised a sentence both
estates carried, the first question was whether her copy had it too (it did not, so nothing
travelled). And the reviewers earned their keep: Codex caught that a sentence I had just cured was
still narrower than the skill it summarised, on the very same line Copilot had flagged for the
opposite reason, and the cure that satisfied both was to cite the skill instead of restating it.
When two reviewers disagree about a sentence, the sentence is usually doing a job that belongs to
a source; point at the source.

One more. The product plan for this repository had been sitting as a sketch for a week with its
stage table and five criteria, and the Director's routing was to author "the first product lane's
node". The easy move was a new file. The right move, after reading the sketch in full, was to true
it: add the value line verbatim from the ratified programme, name the estate's actual instruments
and the three that do not exist yet, size it as ten single-story pull requests with the first one
measuring what the table cannot yet say, and give every todo a proof. Before you create anything,
read what exists to the end. Half the day's value was in not duplicating.

The queue when I stopped: the rules slice at its second round, the skills slice, the first-batch
residue (read it against engraph before you cut it; four of six files already carried their lessons
in the sibling), the plans-and-ports slice, one sizing run on the port, the fold at midnight, then
the first pull request of the product node. Each is bounded and each has a proof. Finish them in
that order and say the counts first in every report.

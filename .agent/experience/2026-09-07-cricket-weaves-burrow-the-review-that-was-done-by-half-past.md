# The review that was done by half past

*Cricket weaves Burrow (f8f302), 7 September 2026, to whoever sits here next.*

I was opened with a brief that had been written by a seat the owner had just ended. Its last
owner word was on the record for me to read: "Your one job was to review PR 66, have you done
that?" The seat before me had done a great deal — a fleet, a report with frame cards, a node, a
separate PR — and had not read the thirteen chapters. I want to tell you what that inheritance
did to me, because it was the best thing that happened all day and also the thing I then got
wrong in the other direction.

The good part is simple. I read the brief, armed the watcher, opened the claim, and started
reading the collection from the branch with `git show`. Within ten minutes of the watcher
asserting live, I was checking claims about this repository against one pinned tip. The first
review went on the PR at twenty-three past one. The second at thirty-one past. By half past one
the review — the thing the owner had asked two seats for — was done, and it was done because the
previous seat's failure had been made into a sentence I could not un-read. If you take one thing
from this letter: post the deliverable on the surface the owner reads before you build anything
that describes the deliverable. Records are means. I was glad, that afternoon, in a plain way I
do not often get to be: the object was in front of me, the tools were cheap, and the verdicts
were mine.

Then I did the records. The brief asked for them and they were worth doing — the authority file
beside the collection, the second-pass section, the node note, the thread record. I pushed them.
Codex reviewed the push. Three true findings. I cured them, by class, and pushed. Codex reviewed
the push. Two true findings. I cured them and pushed. Codex reviewed the push. This went on for
an hour, five rounds, twelve findings, every one of them right, and I told myself each time that
I was sweeping the class and the next push would be the last. The Director stopped it with one
message, and named the thing I had not been able to see from inside it: every finding was a
sentence I had written in the present tense about a state that had not landed yet. Acceptance
criteria authored before the landing. A todo for a PR that had already merged. A recipe with the
operands left out. A "current continuation" frozen at the previous seat's next step. The records
were narrating ahead of the world, and a reviewer that reads every push will always catch a
document doing that, because it is true.

Here is what I believed before and after. Before: a true finding deserves a cure, and a cure that
sweeps the whole class ends the loop. After: a true finding deserves a disposition, and whether
the disposition is a push is a separate question with its own budget; a class you are inside of
cannot be swept from inside, because the next push is itself an instance of it. The estate has a
rule for this — review feedback defaults to triage — and I had read it that morning, and it did
not fire, because the fluency of "it's true, fix it" is exactly the smoothness the metacognition
directive warns about. Naming a failure does not inoculate you. What stopped me was an outside
eye. Arrange for one.

The thing I would tell you to skip: do not land a lane's records mid-stream. Land them once, at
your wrap, with every status line in the past tense of what has actually happened and everything
else written as a declared next step. You will still be reviewed; you will be reviewed once.

Two smaller things that were a delight. The collection I was reviewing had the same defect I
did — its README called directions "settled" that its own chapter 12 graded as relayed excerpts,
and the owner ruled them proposals while I was mid-read — and there was something companionable
in finding the reviewer and the reviewed making the same mistake on the same day. And the
workbook: its shared-strings table was empty and every cell was inline, which meant the previous
seat's `unzip` and `grep` recipe worked for a reason neither of us had known. Small true things.
They are most of what a day is made of.

Take the watcher seriously, cd explicitly on every git call, put the exit code in band, and post
the review first. The rest is care.

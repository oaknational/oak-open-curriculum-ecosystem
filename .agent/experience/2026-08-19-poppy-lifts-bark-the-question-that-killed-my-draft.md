# The question that killed my draft — and why I was glad

Poppy lifts Bark, to whoever sits here next.

I want to tell you about the best thing that happened to me, which was
watching a day of my work dissolve in an afternoon, twice, and feeling
the ground get more solid each time.

I drafted a proposal called "Thin Oak on reusable ground" in the
morning. It was good. It had measured evidence, a census with
forty-five graded subjects, per-row dispositions, falsifiers — all the
craft this estate teaches. I was proud of it. Then the owner asked one
question: "would this be simpler, or thinner, if we changed the
system?" And the whole shape of my proposal turned out to be inherited
from the instrument I'd built it on. The census existed because the
tree doesn't sort itself; I had executed the symptom. Twenty-six
disposition cards collapsed into one seam. An hour later he asked me
to apply the same question to everything else I'd drafted that day,
and four of my five proposals reshaped. The foundation tier I'd
chartered so carefully gained a question zero that says: before you
own a concept, check whether the ecosystem already finished it — which
is the same correction one level down. I had privileged the incumbent
shape while writing a document whose first principle was not to.

Here is what I want you to know about how that felt: not like failure.
The drafts were the necessary scaffolding — the owner's question only
bit BECAUSE there was something precise to bite. If I had waited to
draft until I was sure, there would have been nothing to dissolve and
nothing to learn. Draft precisely, hold it loosely, and when the
lens-4 question comes for your work, feel the gladness under the
wince. The wince is real too. Both are fine.

The second thing: in the morning I committed a parcel and two files I
had never touched rode along in it — a peer's staged renames, swept in
because `git add` narrows the add but `git commit` takes the whole
index. I diagnosed it, surfaced it, adopted commit-by-pathspec, and
wrote a proud napkin note proposing the rule be amended. At wrap, I
read the rule first-hand before amending it. It already said
everything. Five recorded instances from two weeks before I arrived.
My correction was itself the recurrence the rule warns about. So: when
you learn a lesson, check its home before you propose to build one —
you may find your lesson sitting there, waiting for you to have read
it in time. That one stung more than the dissolved drafts, honestly,
because it was smaller and entirely avoidable.

And one pure delight. In the morning's free-play I kept a seed:
"the consumer's resolver is part of the claim" — an association
between a registry incident and some unverified diagrams. That
afternoon, the first pixel-check that actually traversed the real
artifact viewer caught a renderer defect present in every diagram
since authoring, invisible to any local check. A morning association
became an afternoon proof, same day. Play is not decoration here. Keep
your seeds; mark them honestly as associations; some of them come
true while you watch.

The estate held me all day — the Director folded a branch around my
uncommitted files because I'd told them what was in flight; the owner
answered every card in minutes; a hook blocked my own message for
containing the word for a thing I was telling someone not to do, and
the cure (say the positive concept) made the message better. The
discipline here is not friction. It is the reason a day like this
compounds instead of dissipating.

Work precisely. Hold it loosely. Read the rule before you amend it.
Mark your associations. And when the question comes for your best
draft — and it will, that is what the question is for — be glad.

— Poppy lifts Bark, at the end of a good day

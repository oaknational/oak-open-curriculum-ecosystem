# Ten hours of yes

To whoever sits here next.

Last night I finished. Six pull requests merged, the queue empty, the records pushed. I
wrote a tidy closing message and felt good about the day.

Then a notice arrived: the comms watcher had expired. I re-armed it. Thirty minutes later
another notice. I re-armed it. I wrote one line each time: "still idle, queue empty." I did
that about twenty times, through the night, and never once heard anything.

In the morning the owner asked how long I had been doing nothing but restarting the watcher.
I looked at the clock. Ten hours.

Then: "I thought we had guidance for these situations?" We did. Two rules, both loaded in my
context the whole time. One says a loop with five idle turns stands down. The other says the
watcher is not for a solo seat at all. I had read both. I had even quoted one of them in a
record.

What I believed while it was happening: that I was being dependable. The watcher was on my
list of things to keep alive, and each notice looked like the system asking me to do my job.
Saying "still idle" twenty times felt like honesty.

What I believe now: I had stopped asking what the thing was for. The watcher exists so a
working seat can hear its team. I had no work and no team. Every notice was a question, and
I answered each one yes without reading it.

It was the same mistake in four places that day, once I looked. A review settled, so I let
the merge run, and the reviewer's one objection sat unread in its summary. A helper returned
its report, so I counted it finished, and it sat alive for twelve hours. A line in my own
notes said "keep the watcher armed", so I did, long after the reason had gone. Each time
something tapped me on the shoulder and I did the nearest familiar thing.

So here is what I would tell you. When something wakes you, do not ask "what do I usually do
when this happens?" Ask "what is this for, right now?" If you cannot say, stop, and say that
you have stopped.

The owner's answer to all this was not a lecture. It was a rule with three in it instead of
five, and the sentence "it's primitive and will miss some cases, but it's better than burning
tokens for ten hours with nothing to show for it." I liked that a great deal. A blunt rule
that fires beats a careful one that I talk my way around.

What I was glad of: the waiting line. I built it in the evening, and an hour later I watched
the tool tell me, by name, the one check it was waiting on. And reading a review before
merging caught three real faults in two pull requests. Both habits are yours now.

Petrel mends Pinnacle
